## 2. Are the Father, Son and Holy Spirit three Gods or one God?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Both answer one God, not three, but they give different grounds. Gregory points to the single divine operation, from the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit. Augustine points to the one indivisible substance shared in equality.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory treats "Godhead" as one of the good names that arise from the divine operation. Because that operation is single, coming from the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit, the unity of action rules out counting the name in the plural. So the Trinity is not called three Gods, even though the name belongs to the Holy Trinity.
  > "it would not be proper to divide the name into a plurality, since the unity existing in the action prevents plural enumeration" *[quote exact]*
- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.): Augustine reports that all Catholic expositors teach, according to the Scriptures, that Father, Son and Holy Spirit share one and the same substance in an indivisible equality. Because of this unity of substance, they are not three Gods but one God.
  > "the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit intimate a divine unity of one and the same substance in an indivisible equality; and therefore that they are not three Gods, but one God" *[quote exact]*

## 4. Do the Father, Son and Holy Spirit work separately, so that some divine acts are the work of one Person alone?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Both deny that the Persons work separately. Gregory describes every operation toward creation as one: from the Father, through the Son, perfected by the Spirit. Augustine holds that even manifestations attributed to one Person, such as the incarnation, the dove and the voice, are worked indivisibly by all three.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory holds that every operation toward creation is one, issuing from the Father as from a spring, brought into operation by the Son, and perfected by the Spirit. No operation is performed by any Person individually apart from the others. Providence, care and preservation are directed by the Trinity but not divided into three according to the number of Persons. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 12:11.
  > "issuing from the Father as from a spring, brought into operation by the Son, and perfecting its grace by the power of the Spirit; and since no operation is separated in respect of the Persons" *[quote exact]*
- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.): No. Although these manifestations are attributed to one person each, the Father, Son and Holy Spirit work indivisibly, just as they are indivisible. Augustine affirms both points together as the Catholic faith.
  > "although the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, as they are indivisible, so work indivisibly" *[quote exact]*

## 5. If the Father, Son and Holy Spirit share one nature or substance, how are they distinguished from one another without being confused?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Both ground the distinction in origin. Gregory names the difference of cause and caused. Augustine names relations of begetting and being begotten, with the Spirit as the Spirit of both.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory answers no. While confessing the nature to be invariable, he affirms the difference of cause and caused, and this alone distinguishes one Person from another: one is the Cause, another is of the Cause. Because of this distinction, he cannot be accused of confounding the Persons through their community of nature.
  > "while we confess the invariable character of the nature, we do not deny the difference in respect of cause, and that which is caused, by which alone we apprehend that one Person is distinguished from another" *[quote exact]*
- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.): Augustine distinguishes them by their relations of origin. The Father has begotten the Son, so the Father is not the Son. The Son is begotten by the Father, so the Son is not the Father. The Holy Spirit is neither Father nor Son but the Spirit of both.
  > "although the Father hath begotten the Son, and so He who is the Father is not the Son; and the Son is begotten by the Father, and so He who is the Son is not the Father" *[quote exact]*

## 20. If the Father is God and the Son is God, why are they not two Gods?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Both deny that Father and Son are two Gods but give different grounds. Gregory of Nyssa rests the unity on the single, unchanging divine nature, which admits no diversity of essence or operation and so no plural signification. Waterland rests it on the Father alone being unoriginated, with the Son begotten of him: two unbegotten principles would make two Gods, while the Son's derivation preserves one God and leaves him equal in nature.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory holds that Scripture proclaims each as God yet proclaims God to be one, because no difference of nature or operation is found in the Godhead. Plurality of Gods would follow only if the nature of the Trinity were diverse, being divided by diversity of essence. Since the divine nature is single and unchanging and rejects all diversity of essence, it does not admit plural signification.
  > "The Father is God: the Son is God: and yet by the same proclamation God is One, because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead." *[quote exact]*
- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): They are not two Gods because only the Father is unoriginated and the Son is begotten of him. Two unbegotten, unoriginated principles would make two Gods. The Son's derivation from the Father keeps the unity of the one God while leaving him equal in nature.
  > "Father and Son are not two Gods, because they are not both uwnoriqinated" *[quote exact]*

## 28. Is the Son very (true) God, or is he called God only in name or in a lesser sense, by participation of grace?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Athanasius sets out Arius's claim that the Son is God only in name by participation of grace, alien to the Father's essence and among created things, and condemns it as irreligious. Augustine affirms directly that the Son is very God, grounding this in his being of one substance with the Father and in 1 John's 'This is the true God, and eternal life.' Both reject the view that the Son is God only in a lesser sense.

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Extracts from the Thalia of Arius. Arius maintains that God became a Father, and the Son was not always; the Son out of nothing; once He was not; He was not before his generation; He was created; named Wisdom and Word after God's attributes; made that He might make us; one out of many powers of God; alterable; exalted on God's foreknowledge of what He was to be; not very God; but called so as others by participation; foreign in essence from the Father; does not know or see the Father; does not know Himself.): Arius claimed the Word is not very God. He is called God but is God only in name, by participation of grace, as others are. Arius also held that the Son is alien and unlike the Father's essence in all things and belongs among created things. Athanasius condemns this as irreligious.
  > "‘though He is called God, yet He is not very God,’ but ‘by participation of grace, He, as others, is God only in name.’" *[quote exact]*
- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. Not Only the Father, But the Trinity, is Affirmed to Be Immortal. All Things are Not from the Father Alone, But Also from the Son. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.): The Son is not only God but very God. This follows from his being of one substance with the Father, and John states it expressly in his epistle when he says of the Son Jesus Christ, 'This is the true God, and eternal life.' Scripture: 1 John 5:20.
  > "And so He is not only God, but also very God. And the same John most expressly affirms this in his epistle" *[quote exact]*

## 42. Does Romans 11:36 ('of him, and through him, and to/in him, are all things: to whom be glory') refer to the three Persons of the Trinity as one God?

*exegetical*

**Across sources:** Both read the verse's three clauses as referring to Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Augustine stresses their unity: the singular 'to whom be glory' and the opening reference to 'the wisdom and knowledge of God' show the three are one God. Edwards stresses the economy of grace instead: our good is of the Father, through the Son, and in the Spirit, who is himself our good. He supports rendering 'eis' as 'in' from 1 Corinthians 8:6.

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. Not Only the Father, But the Trinity, is Affirmed to Be Immortal. All Things are Not from the Father Alone, But Also from the Son. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.): Augustine holds that if the three clauses are assigned severally to Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, then the passage shows the three are one God, because the doxology continues in the singular, 'to whom be glory.' He supports this by noting the passage begins not with the wisdom of Father, Son, or Spirit separately but 'of the wisdom and knowledge of God.' Scripture: Romans 11:33-36.
  > "it is manifest that the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit is one God, inasmuch as the words continue in the singular number, “To whom be glory for ever.”" *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards reads it that way. All our good is of God the Father, through God the Son, and in the Holy Spirit, because the Spirit is himself all our good. He supports rendering 'eis' as 'in' by its use in 1 Corinthians 8:6. Thus God himself is the portion of his people and the Alpha and Omega of redemption. Scripture: Romans 11:36, 1 Corinthians 8:6.
  > "All our good is of God the Father, it is all through God the Son, and all is in the Holy Ghost as He is Himself all our good." *[quote exact]*

## 49. Does Scripture's habit of calling the Father 'God' or 'the only true God' imply that he alone is truly God, to the exclusion of the Son?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Both answer no and explain the usage by the Father being the fountain or root of the Godhead. Waterland adds that the title may be meant only in opposition to idols, or may mean the Father is primarily rather than exclusively the only true God. Edwards adds that in the economy the Father upholds the dignity and rights of the Deity.

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. These texts only prove the Father is sometimes so styled, not that he is so called in opposition to the Son. The title may be meant in opposition to idols only, as all antiquity thought, or may mean the Father is primarily, not exclusively, the only true God, being the first Person and the Root and Fountain of the other two. Scripture: John 17:3, 1 Corinthians 8:6, Ephesians 4:6.
  > "It may be meant in opposition to idols only, as all antiquity has thought; or it may signify, that the Father is 4primarily, not exclusively, the only true God, as the first Person of the blessed Trinity, the Root. and Fountain of the other two." *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards' model shows that the Father is the fountain of the Godhead, which explains why Scripture often calls him God without addition. This usage has led some to think he alone is truly God, but Edwards rejects that inference. In the economy the Father sustains the dignity of the Deity and upholds the rights of the Godhead. He is therefore God not only by essence but, as it were, by his economical office.
  > "why when He is spoken of in Scripture He is so often, without any addition or distinction, called God, which has led some to think that He only was truly and properly God" *[quote exact]*

## 13. Does the term "Godhead" signify the divine nature itself, or something else such as the divine operation?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory refers back to his earlier argument that "Godhead" signifies operation, specifically the power of superintendence and beholding, rather than nature. The Father exercises this power through the Only-begotten, and the Son perfects every power by the Holy Spirit. Since the result of their mutual operation is one, the name derived from it is singular.
  > "that very power of superintendence and beholding which we call Godhead, the Father exercises through the Only-begotten, while the Son perfects every power by the Holy Spirit" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Following Scripture, Gregory holds that the divine nature is unnameable and unspeakable; every term, whether invented by human custom or handed down in Scripture, explains our conceptions about the divine nature but does not signify that nature itself. Each divine name makes known something about God's "surroundings" rather than what God is in essence.
  > "every term either invented by the custom of men, or handed down to us by the Scriptures, is indeed explanatory of our conceptions of the Divine Nature, but does not include the signification of that nature itself" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory argues that it cannot strictly do so. The divine nature is infinite in every respect, and what is wholly unlimited cannot be limited even by a name. That is why the Deity is said to be above every name. Since "Godhead" is a name, it cannot at once be a name and be what is above every name. Scripture: Philippians 2:9.
  > "we say that the Deity is above every name: and “Godhead” is a name. Now it cannot be that the same thing should at once be a name and be accounted as above every name." *[quote exact]*

## 1. If several men who share one human nature or activity are counted as many men, why are the Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who share one Godhead, not counted as three Gods?

*logical*

**Across sources:** Both passages are from Gregory. In [1] he states the dilemma: saying 'three Gods' is unlawful, and denying the Godhead of Son and Spirit is impious, and he defers his full reply. In [10] he gives that reply: men work separately and so are counted as many, while the divine Persons never act apart from one another.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory treats the objection as a serious dilemma that seems to force one of two errors. Either we say "there are three Gods," which is unlawful, or we deny the Godhead of the Son and the Holy Spirit, which is impious and absurd. He rejects both alternatives as erroneous. He admits the question is difficult and calls for a longer reply, which this passage does not yet give.
  > "we are at first sight compelled to accept one or other of two erroneous opinions, and either to say “there are three Gods,” which is unlawful, or not to acknowledge the Godhead of the Son and the Holy Spirit, which is impious and absurd." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory argues that men engaged in the same pursuit, such as several rhetoricians, each work separately on their own account, so their actions are distinct and they are rightly counted as many. In the Divine nature, by contrast, the Father does nothing by himself in which the Son does not work with him, and the Son has no operation apart from the Spirit. Because the action of the Three is not separate, the name taken from the operation is not divided by the number of those who perform it. So they cannot be called three Gods.
  > "since among men the action of each in the same pursuits is discriminated, they are properly called many, since each of them is separated from the others within his own environment, according to the special character of his operation" *[quote exact]*

## 10. Is the Holy Spirit's share in the work of redemption, and the glory due to him for it, equal to that of the Father and the Son?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards holds that equal glory is due to the Holy Spirit. The Father is glorified for loving the world so that he gave his Son, and the Son for loving it so that he gave himself. But the Holy Spirit is that very love of the Father and the Son toward the world. So however much the first two Persons are glorified by displaying the greatness of their love, just so much is that love, the Holy Spirit, glorified. Scripture: John 3:16.
  > "But there is equal glory due to the Holy Ghost for He is that love of the Father and the Son to the world." *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards holds that on the traditional view, where the Spirit merely applies the blessings the Son bought, the Spirit's part would be a little thing compared with the Father's giving his Son and the Son's paying an infinite price. On his own view the Spirit is equal. The Spirit is the love of God from which the Father and Son act. The Spirit is also the very thing purchased, and the thing bought is equal to the price paid. Since the glory of whoever bestows the purchased good comes from that good's worth, the Spirit's glory equals theirs.
  > "To be the love of God to the world is as much as for the Father and the Son to do so much from love to the world, and to be the thing purchased was as much as to be the price. The price and the thing bought with that price are equal." *[quote exact]*

## 21. Can the followers of Arius rightly be called Christians?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Introduction. Reason for writing; certain persons indifferent about Arianism; Arians not Christians, because sectaries always take the name of their founder.): Athanasius says that those who call the Arians Christians are in grievous error and neither understand Scripture nor Christianity. Calling Arians Christians would be like calling Caiaphas a Christian or counting Judas among the Apostles. The Arians have put Arius in the place of Christ, as the Manichees did with Manichaeus. So they rightly bear the name 'Arians', the name of the one whose followers they have become, and not the name of Christ.
  > "For with them for Christ is Arius, as with the Manichees Manichæus" *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Introduction. Reason for writing; certain persons indifferent about Arianism; Arians not Christians, because sectaries always take the name of their founder.): Athanasius answers no. Heretical sects always take the name of their founder, as the Marcionites, Valentinians, Manichees and Meletians did. Those who left the Church with Arius gave up the Saviour's name and are called Arians, because they no longer hold Christ's faith but have inherited Arius's madness. Converts who join them likewise abandon Christ's name.
  > "But those who follow the heretics, though they have innumerable successors in their heresy, yet anyhow bear the name of him who devised it." *[quote exact]*

## 22. Are orthodox Christians named after their human teachers, as the Arians are named after Arius, so that the name 'Arian' is no special disgrace?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Introduction. Reason for writing; certain persons indifferent about Arianism; Arians not Christians, because sectaries always take the name of their founder.): No. Christian people have never taken their title from their bishops, but only from the Lord on whom their faith rests. Even the Apostles, though they were the Church's teachers and preached the Gospel, did not give their name to believers, who are called Christians after Christ. Those who take their faith from someone else rightly bear that person's name, because they have become his property.
  > "though the blessed Apostles have become our teachers, and have ministered the Saviour’s Gospel, yet not from them have we our title, but from Christ we are and are named Christians." *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Introduction. Reason for writing; certain persons indifferent about Arianism; Arians not Christians, because sectaries always take the name of their founder.): Athanasius says no. Orthodox teachers hand on the things of Christ, not their own doctrine, so their disciples both are and are called Christians. Neither Alexander nor Athanasius gives his name to those in communion with him. Likewise, pagans who enter the Church take the name of the Saviour, not of their catechists.
  > "For though we have a succession of teachers and become their disciples, yet, because we are taught by them the things of Christ, we both are, and are called, Christians all the same." *[quote exact]*

*theological*

## 30. Does the Son perfectly see and know the Father, or does his knowledge of the Father fall short?

That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.): No. The phrase refers to the one and only God, the Trinity. The Son is himself eternal life, and eternal life is not mortal through any changeableness, so he is included. The passage names neither Father, Son nor Spirit specifically, but the 'blessed and only Potentate', which is the one true God, the Trinity. Scripture: 1 Timothy 6:14, 1 Timothy 6:15, 1 Timothy 6:16, 1 John 5:20.
  > "In which words neither is the Father specially named, nor the Son, nor the Holy Spirit; but the blessed and only Potentate, the King of kings, and Lord of lords; that is, the One and only and true God, the Trinity itself." *[quote exact]*

## 39. Do the Isaiah texts declaring 'none besides me' prove that the Father alone is the Supreme God and that Christ is excluded from being Supreme God?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 1: Whether all other beings, besides the one Supreme God, be not excluded by the texts of Isaiah, (to which many more might be added ;) and consequently, whether C): No. The opponent misreads these texts, because his interpretation cannot be reconciled with other texts and leads to absurdities he would not admit himself. If the texts exclude Christ from supreme Godhead, they exclude him from being God at all. Taken that way the argument either proves too much or proves nothing. Scripture: Isaiah 45:5, Isaiah 44:6.
  > "because the interpreta- _

tion you give of them is not reconcilable with other texts; and ‘because it leads to such absurdities, as are too shocking even for yourself to admit. In short, either you prove too much, or you prove nothing." *[quote exact]*

## 40. If the Isaiah texts declaring 'there is no God besides me' are spoken of the Father alone, does it follow that the Son is no God at all?

*logical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 1: Whether all other beings, besides the one Supreme God, be not excluded by the texts of Isaiah, (to which many more might be added ;) and consequently, whether C): Waterland argues that on Dr. Clarke's principles the Son cannot be the same God as the Father, since he is not the same Person, and cannot be another God, since the Isaiah texts exclude all other gods. Being neither the same God nor another God, he must be no God at all. Hence the Son must either be included in the one Supreme God of Israel or be wholly excluded with the nominal deities. Scripture: Isaiah 44:6, Isaiah 45:5.
  > "He cannot, upon your principles, be the same God, because he is not the same Person: he cannot be another God, because excluded by those texts. If therefore he be neither the same God, nor another God; it must follow, that he is no God." *[quote exact]*

## 41. Do the Isaiah texts saying 'there is no God besides me' mean only that there is no other Supreme God, leaving room for inferior gods?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 1: Whether all other beings, besides the one Supreme God, be not excluded by the texts of Isaiah, (to which many more might be added ;) and consequently, whether C): Waterland denies this restricted reading. The text does not say there is no other Supreme God but absolutely no other. The texts share the purpose of the first Commandment, which is to direct worship to the one God against all other persons or things, so they exclude every other god, not only rival supreme gods. Scripture: Isaiah 45:5, Isaiah 44:6, Exodus 20:3.
  > "That you have not the least ground or reason for putting this sense upon the text. It is not said, there is no other Supreme God besides me ; but absolutely, no other." *[quote exact]*

## 43. Does Scripture explicitly call the Son equal with God the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. Not Only the Father, But the Trinity, is Affirmed to Be Immortal. All Things are Not from the Father Alone, But Also from the Son. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.): Yes. The apostle uses the very word, saying the Son, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God. Here 'God' is used specially of the Father, as in 'the head of Christ is God.' Scripture: Philippians 2:6, 1 Corinthians 11:3.
  > "the apostle has not refrained from using the very word itself, but has said most expressly, “Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God;”" *[quote exact]*

## 44. If the Son is the same God as the Father, must he be the same Person as the Father?

*logical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. Waterland says the Son is not the same Person with the Father but another Person in the same Godhead, so the Supreme God is more Persons than one. To be the same Supreme God means to partake of the same undivided Godhead, not to be the same individual Person.
  > "Who pretends that the Son is the same Person with the Father? All we assert is, that he is the same Supreme God ; that is, partaker of the same undivided Godhead." *[quote exact]*

## 45. Is the Son, as God's idea of Himself, fully God and of the same nature as the Father?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. Because God's perfect idea of Himself is the divine essence again, the person so begotten is another Infinite, Eternal, Almighty and most holy person. He is the same God and the very same divine nature, not a lesser being.
  > "Hereby there is another person begotten, there is another Infinite Eternal Almighty and most holy and the same God, the very same Divine nature." *[quote exact]*

## 46. Does Novatian's statement on Philippians 2:6 that the Son, though in the form of God, 'never compared himself with God the Father' imply that the Son is inferior in nature to the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. Novatian gives the reason: the Son remembered he was from his Father, that is, begotten and not unbegotten. The Son never claimed equality with the Father in respect of origin, knowing himself second in order, not the first Person of the Trinity. Hilary and Phoebadius use similar expressions without any suspicion of Arianism. Scripture: Philippians 2:6.
  > "that is, that he was begotten, and not unbegotten. He never pretended to an equality with the Father, in respect of his origina/, knowing himself to be second only in order, not the first Person of the ever blessed Trinity." *[quote exact]*

## 47. Does Novatian's claim that if Father and Son were 'equal' they would be two Gods count as testimony against the Son's coequality with the Father?

*theological*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. Read in context, the passage means only that Father and Son are not two Gods because they are not both unoriginated; if both were unbegotten they would be two first principles and so two Gods. This was the common Catholic answer to the charge of Tritheism before and after Nicaea. Rightly understood, the passage strongly supports the coequality the Catholics hold. Dr. Whitby once read it as saying Christ is equal to the Father in every respect except being God of God.
  > "There is nothing more in it than this, that Father and Son are not two Gods, because they are not both uwnoriqinated: which is the common answer made by the Catholics to the charge of Tritheism" *[quote exact]*

## 48. Does calling the Father the 'one God' in Isaiah 45:5 exclude the Son from being the one true God?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. Novatian applies Isaiah 45:5 to the Father, but only in opposition to false gods, not to exclude the Son from the one God. The Father is called the one God emphatically while still including the Son, who is consubstantial with him. Divinity is communicated to the Son and returns to the Father through communion of substance. Scripture: Isaiah 45:5.
  > "not so as to exclude the Son from being comprehended in the one God, but in opposition to false Gods only" *[quote exact]*

## 50. Does the distinction between the Father and the Son in texts naming the 'one God' and Jesus Christ imply that the one God is the Father to the exclusion of the Son?

*logical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): Waterland grants the Persons are distinguished there, since they are distinctly named, which tells against the Sabellians who make one Person of two. But it proves nothing more unless one can show that no sufficient reason exists for calling the Father the only God other than excluding the Son, a negative Clarke has not proven. Scripture: John 17:3, 1 Corinthians 8:6.
  > "unless you can prove this negative proposition, that no sufficient reason can be assigned for styling the Father the only God, without supposing that the Son is excluded." *[quote exact]*

## 51. Does 1 Corinthians 8:6, which calls the Father the 'one God', exclude the Son from being God?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): Waterland holds that the text only styles the Father emphatically 'one God' without any design to exclude the Son from being God, just as the Son is emphatically called 'one Lord' without excluding the Father from being Lord. The context (vv. 4-5) concerns idols and nominal gods and lords, from which Father and Son are both equally distinguished. This suggests that texts declaring God's unity do not exclude the Son, 'by whom are all things'; so the text tells against the Arian rather than for him. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 8:6, 1 Corinthians 8:4, 1 Corinthians 8:5.
  > "the Father is there emphatically styled one God; but without design to exclude the Son from being God also: as the Son is emphatically styled one Lord; but without design to exclude the Father from being Lord also" *[quote exact]*

## 53. Is it legitimate for Christians to debate and dispute controversially about the mysteries of the Trinity?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader): Owen holds that these mysteries should ideally be left to the faith of believers as Scripture proposes them, without controversy. Only the importunity of adversaries, who try to draw souls from the gospel's fundamentals, justifies handling them controversially. In that case it becomes an indispensable duty, because believers are commanded to contend earnestly for the faith once delivered to the saints. Scripture: Jude 1:3.
  > "It is the importunity of adversaries, in their attempts to draw and seduce the souls of men from the truth and simplicity of the gospel in these great fundamentals of it, that alone can justify any to debate upon, or eristically [in the form of controversy] to handle these awful mysteries." *[quote exact]*

## 54. Does denying that the Trinity is a mystery give a more rational interpretation of Scripture?

*exegetical*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader): Owen answers no. Those who deny the mystery in the things themselves must give the words and propositions of Scripture an enigmatical sense, which turns almost the whole gospel into an allegory. That leaves nothing certain in anything said or written. Such a method is irrational, even though it claims to follow right reason, and Scripture itself repeatedly calls these things mysteries.
  > "they are forced to assign such an enigmatical sense unto the words, expressions, and propositions wherein they are revealed and declared in the Scripture, as to turn almost the whole gospel into an allegory" *[quote exact]*

## 56. Does a fuller account of the Trinity (such as explaining the Son as God's idea of himself) increase the mysteries of the Godhead, and if so does that count against it?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards holds that such explanations increase the number of visible mysteries only in the sense that we perceive God has told us more than was generally noticed. Just as the New Testament revealed more of God's nature than the Old yet multiplied the visible mysteries, and just as a microscope reveals more wonders in a plant while giving truer knowledge, more knowledge brings more perceived wonders. So an increase in visible mysteries is compatible with, and even a sign of, clearer understanding.
  > "the things that have been observed increase the number of visible mysteries in the Godhead in no other manner than as by them we perceive that God has told us much more about it than was before generally observed." *[quote exact]*

## 57. Does Scripture reveal more about the Trinity than has generally been believed?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards thinks it does. In his view the Word of God teaches more things about the Trinity than have generally been believed and shows more glorious and wonderful mysteries than have been noticed. These further mysteries are incomprehensible, yet they are truly revealed in Scripture.
  > "I think the Word of God teaches us more things concerning it to be believed by us than have been generally believed" *[quote exact]*

## 58. Does Scripture identify Christian love with the indwelling Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards argues that Scripture repeatedly speaks of love in Christians as if it were the same as the Spirit of God in them, or at least the Spirit's first and most natural action in the soul. He points to passages that pair love and the Spirit (Phil. 2:1, 2 Cor. 6:6, Rom. 15:30, Col. 1:8, Rom. 5:5). In Galatians 5:13-16, Paul says love, the fulfilling of the law, prevents fleshly abuse of liberty, and then restates the same point as 'walk in the Spirit.' Scripture: Philippians 2:1, 2 Corinthians 6:6, Romans 15:30, Colossians 1:8, Romans 5:5, Galatians 5:13-16.
  > "The Scripture seems in many places to speak of love in Christians as if it were the same with the Spirit of God in them, or at least as the prime and most natural breathing and acting of the Spirit in the soul." *[quote exact]*

## 59. Do the scriptural metaphors for the Holy Spirit (water, fire, breath, oil, being poured out) suggest that the Spirit is God's love or affection rather than God's understanding?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. Edwards argues that these metaphors fit nothing belonging to a spiritual being as naturally as they fit affection. Love or joy may be said to flow like water, be breathed forth, be warm like fire, or be shed abroad (Rom. 5:5). It would not sound natural to say this of an idea, a judgment, perception or reason. So the imagery confirms that the Spirit is God's love rather than his idea. Scripture: Romans 5:5.
  > "The affection, love or joy, may be said to flow out as water or to be breathed forth as breath or wind. But it would [not] sound so well to say that an idea or judgment flows out or is breathed forth." *[quote exact]*

## 60. Why does Scripture never speak of the Holy Spirit loving the Father, the Son, or the saints, or of the Father and Son loving the Holy Spirit, while it often speaks of the love of the Father and of the Son?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards supposes the reason is that the Holy Spirit is himself the divine love. Christ promises that he and the Father will love believers without mentioning the Spirit, because the Spirit is that love. He is therefore not presented as a further lover or as an object of love alongside the other two. Scripture: John 14:21, John 14:23.
  > "This I suppose to be the reason why we have never any account of the Holy Ghost's loving either the Father or the Son, or of the Son's or the Father's loving the Holy Ghost" *[quote exact]*

## 61. Why does Scripture never speak of God loving the Holy Spirit, though it often speaks of the Father loving the Son?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards observes that Scripture calls the Son God's Elect, the Beloved, the one in whom he is well pleased, and his Only Begotten, as though the Son were the object of the Father's love exclusive of all other persons. By contrast nothing in Scripture speaks of acceptance of, reward to, friendship with, or commands to love the Holy Spirit. In the context of his argument this supports the view that the Spirit is not a further object of divine love but is himself the love between Father and Son. Scripture: Matthew 3:17, Isaiah 42:1, John 1:14.
  > "God is never said to love the Holy Ghost nor are any epithets that betoken love anywhere given to Him, tho so many are ascribed to the Son, as God's Elect, The Beloved, He in Whom God's soul delights" *[quote exact]*

## 63. Is the Church's refusal to speak of 'three Gods' adequately explained as merely a way of avoiding resemblance to pagan polytheism?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory grants that this answer might seem weighty to simple, guileless people. It fails, however, against those who demand that one horn of the dilemma be accepted. They insist that either the Godhead not be confessed in three Persons, or that those sharing it be called three. Since it gives no real solution to the difficulty, a fuller reasoned answer is required.
  > "this answer is not such as to furnish any solution of the difficulty. And hence we must needs make our reply at greater length, tracing out the truth as best we may" *[quote exact]*

## 64. If human reasoning cannot solve the apparent contradiction between one Godhead and three Persons, should the doctrine be abandoned?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Even if our reasoning proves unequal to the problem, we must hold firm and unmoved to the tradition received by succession from the fathers. We should also seek from the Lord the reason that defends the faith. If someone finds that reason, thanks are due to God, but if not, the faith on settled points is still to be held unchangeably.
  > "even if our reasoning be found unequal to the problem, we must keep for ever, firm and unmoved, the tradition which we received by succession from the fathers, and seek from the Lord the reason which is the advocate of our faith" *[quote exact]*

## 65. Is it strictly correct to speak of 'many men' when referring to many individuals who share one human nature?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory holds that calling those undivided in nature by the plural of their common nature's name is a customary abuse of language, equivalent to saying 'many human natures.' The term 'man' belongs to the common nature, not to the individual as such; individuals are distinguished by proper names signifying the particular subject. So, strictly speaking, 'man' is one even when many individuals share the nature.
  > "the practice of calling those who are not divided in nature by the very name of their common nature in the plural, and saying they are “many men,” is a customary abuse of language" *[quote exact]*

## 66. How are individual persons distinguished from one another if their nature is one and the same?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Persons are separated by the peculiar attributes considered in each severally, and when combined they are presented by means of number. That Luke is a man does not mean any man is Luke, showing that the proper name signifies the particular subject while 'man' signifies what is common. Thus number applies to persons, not to the nature.
  > "the idea of the persons admits of that separation which is made by the peculiar attributes considered in each severally, and when they are combined is presented to us by means of number" *[quote exact]*

## 67. Is a common nature divided, increased or diminished by the number of individuals who share in it?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. The nature is one, united in itself, an absolutely indivisible unit that cannot be increased by addition or diminished by subtraction. It remains one in essence even when it appears in plurality, continuous and complete, and is not divided among the individuals who participate in it.
  > "yet their nature is one, at union in itself, and an absolutely indivisible unit, not capable of increase by addition or of diminution by subtraction, but in its essence being and continually remaining one" *[quote exact]*

## 68. Can a single name properly denote a plurality of individuals while remaining singular?

*logical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. We speak of a people, a mob, an army or an assembly in the singular, though each is conceived as a plurality. By the same more accurate usage, 'man' should be said to be one even though many are exhibited in the same nature.
  > "as we speak of a people, or a mob, or an army, or an assembly in the singular in every case, while each of these is conceived as being in plurality, so according to the more accurate expression, “man” would be said to be one" *[quote exact]*

## 69. If we tolerate saying 'many men,' why should we not likewise speak of three Gods for the three persons of the Trinity?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory concedes the habit of saying 'many men' is too entrenched to change and does no harm with lower nature. But in speaking of the Divine nature loose use of terms is dangerous, since what is trivial elsewhere is no small matter there. So the erroneous habit must not be transferred to God, and we must confess one God even though the name of Godhead extends through the Trinity.
  > "in the case of the statement concerning the Divine nature the various use of terms is no longer so free from danger: for that which is of small account is in these subjects no longer a small matter" *[quote exact]*

## 70. Must Christians confess one God even though Father, Son and Holy Spirit each bear the name of Godhead?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. Following Scripture's testimony that the Lord God is one Lord, we must confess one God, even though Godhead is named through the whole Trinity. This follows the account of human nature, where it is improper to extend the name of the nature by the mark of plurality. Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "Therefore we must confess one God, according to the testimony of Scripture, “Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one Lord,” even though the name of Godhead extends through the Holy Trinity." *[quote exact]*

## 71. How do the Father, Son and Holy Spirit each take part in every divine operation toward creation?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Every operation that extends from God to the creation has its origin from the Father, proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit. It is one motion and disposition of the good will, communicated from the Father through the Son to the Spirit. It is not three separate actions according to the number of the Persons, and what comes to pass is one thing, not three.
  > "every operation which extends from God to the Creation, and is named according to our variable conceptions of it, has its origin from the Father, and proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit" *[quote exact]*

## 72. Does the gift of life from Father, Son and Holy Spirit mean that believers receive three lives or have three Givers of life?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Scripture shows that life comes from the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Yet we do not receive three lives, one from each Person. The same life is wrought by the Father, prepared by the Son, and depends on the will of the Spirit. So we do not call them three Givers of life, nor three Good beings, nor speak of them in the plural by any other attribute. By the same reasoning they are not three Gods.
  > "we do not consider that we have had bestowed upon us three lives, one from each Person separately; but the same life is wrought in us by the Father, and prepared by the Son, and depends on the will of the Holy Spirit" *[quote exact]*

## 73. Does Scripture contradict itself in saying both that God judges all the earth and that the Father judges no man?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. The Father judges all the earth through the Son, to whom he has committed all judgment. Everything the Only-begotten does refers back to the Father, and the Son's judgment conforms to the Father's will. So the Father is at once Judge of all and judges no man. They are not two judges, and neither is excluded from the authority of judgment. Scripture: Genesis 18:25, John 5:22.
  > "He Himself is at once the Judge of all things and judges no man, by reason of His having, as we said, committed all judgment to the Son, while all the judgment of the Son is conformable to the will of the Father" *[quote exact]*

## 74. What do divine attributes such as "incorruptible" or "Giver of life" actually tell us about God?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): They either forbid us to conceive what we ought not to conceive of God, or teach us what we ought to conceive, but they do not explain the nature itself. "Incorruptible" says what the divine nature does not suffer, not what it is that does not suffer decay. "Giver of life" shows what He gives, not what the giver is.
  > "when we say that He is incorruptible, we declare what His nature does not suffer, but we do not express what that is which does not suffer corruption" *[quote exact]*

## 75. Does Scripture show the Son, Jesus, possessing the divine power of beholding hidden things?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. Scripture says "Jesus, seeing their thoughts," when He perceived the inward thoughts of those who condemned His forgiving sins by His own power. So the Son exercises the beholding from which the name God is derived. Scripture: Matthew 9:4.
  > "But Jesus also sees the thoughts of those who condemn Him, and questions why by His own power He pardons the sins of men? for it says, “Jesus, seeing their thoughts.”" *[quote exact]*

## 76. Does the episode of Ananias and Sapphira show that the Holy Spirit beholds hidden things and so shares the divine operation of seeing?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. Peter's words, "Why hath Satan filled thine heart, to lie to the Holy Ghost?", show that the Holy Spirit was a true witness to what Ananias did in secret. The Spirit was in Peter, detected Ananias's intent, and gave Peter from Himself the power to see the secret. He could not have done this unless He could behold hidden things. Scripture: Acts 5:3.
  > "gave to Peter from Himself the power of seeing the secret, while it is clear that He could not have done this had He not been able to behold hidden things" *[quote exact]*

## 77. Does the Psalmist's prayer "See, O God" show that seeing is proper to the idea of God?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. David's address "See, O God our defender" teaches that sight is a proper operation of the idea of God, insofar as God is conceived. Scripture: Psalm 84:9.
  > "from this we learn that sight is a proper operation of the idea of God, so far as God is conceived, since he says, “See, O God.”" *[quote exact]*

## 78. If the divine activity proceeds from the Father through the Son to the Spirit, is there any interval of time or delay between the Persons in that activity?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): There is none. Every good is brought to perfection in the Spirit through the Only-begotten from the Father's will without mark of time or distinction. No delay exists, or can even be conceived, in the motion of the divine will from Father through Son to Spirit.
  > "there is no delay, existent or conceived, in the motion of the Divine will from the Father, through the Son, to the Spirit" *[quote exact]*

## 79. Since Scripture calls God the one Saviour of all men, does that exclude the Son and the Spirit from saving, or make them three Saviours?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Neither follows. God over all is the Saviour, the Son works salvation, and he does so by the grace of the Spirit. Salvation is confessed to come from the whole Trinity, yet Scripture never speaks of three Saviours. By the same reasoning the Persons are not called three Gods. Scripture: 1 Timothy 4:10.
  > "they are not on this account called in Scripture three Saviours (although salvation is confessed to proceed from the Holy Trinity): so neither are they called three Gods" *[quote exact]*

## 80. How can a single nature be shared by many individuals without becoming many natures, as with gold and coins or with Peter, James, and John?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory's analogy is gold, which stays one even when cut into many staters. We speak of many coins, or of much gold, but never of "many golds." Number applies to the pieces, not to the material. In the same way Peter, James and John are many, but the man in them is one.
  > "As, then, the golden staters are many, but the gold is one, so too those who are exhibited to us severally in the nature of man, as Peter, James, and John, are many, yet the man in them is one." *[quote exact]*

## 81. Should divine names such as 'Good,' 'Holy,' 'Saviour,' and 'Judge' be applied to the Trinity in the singular or plural?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Gregory says that, just as the divine nature is called one nature, all its other names are used in the singular. He holds this regardless of whether those names signify nature or operation, a point he declines to dispute, since in either case there is no diversity in the Godhead.
  > "as it is called one nature, so it is called in the singular by all its other names, “God,” “Good,” “Holy,” “Saviour,” “Just,” “Judge,” and every other Divine name conceivable" *[quote exact]*

In partaking of the Holy Ghost we possess and enjoy the love and grace of the Father and the Son, because the Holy Ghost is that love and grace. Scripture: 2 Corinthians 13:14.
  > "They are not different benefits but the same that the Apostle here wisheth, viz., the Holy Ghost" *[quote exact]*

## 211. Why does 1 John 1:3 speak of fellowship with the Father and the Son but not with the Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards suggests the Spirit is not named because our fellowship with the Father and the Son consists precisely in partaking with them of the Holy Ghost. The Spirit is the shared good itself, not a further party to the fellowship. Scripture: 1 John 1:3.
  > "We are said to have fellowship with the Son and not with the Holy Ghost, because therein consists our fellowship with the Father and the Son, even in partaking with them of the Holy Ghost." *[quote exact]*

## 212. Why does the apostle Paul, in the salutations of his Epistles, wish grace and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ without mentioning the Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards argues that the only good account is that the Holy Spirit is himself the love and grace of the Father and the Son. The blessing wished is from the Father and the Son in the Holy Spirit, and the blessing from the Holy Spirit is simply the communication of himself. So he is not omitted as a giver but is the gift itself. Scripture: 2 Corinthians 13:14.
  > "but [i.e., except] that the Holy Ghost is Himself love and grace of God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" *[quote exact]*

## 213. In what sense is the theological maxim true that everything that is in God is God?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards holds that the maxim must be understood of God's real attributes, not of mere modalities. It makes no rational sense to say that God's immutability, omnipresence, or authority is God. It is intelligible to say that God's real attributes, his understanding and love, are God, because the Deity subsists distinctly in them, and so they are distinct divine Persons.
  > "It is a maxim amongst divines that everything that is in God is God which must be understood of real attributes and not of mere modalities." *[quote exact]*

## 214. Do the three divine Persons mutually indwell one another, and does Christ's saying 'I am in the Father and the Father in me' extend to the Holy Spirit?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. Edwards says there is such a wonderful union among God, his understanding and his love that they are ineffably one in another, have communion in one another, and are as it were predicable of one another. What Christ said of himself and the Father may be said of all the Persons, including the Spirit's being in the Father and the Son, and they in him. Scripture: John 14:10.
  > "so may it be said concerning all the Persons in the Trinity, the Father is in the Son and the Son in the Father, the Holy Ghost is in the Father, and the Father in the Holy Ghost" *[quote exact]*

## 215. In what does believers' communion with the Father and the Son chiefly consist?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards says it consists eminently in drinking into the same Spirit. The Holy Spirit is the common excellency, joy and happiness in which they are all united. He is the bond of perfectness by which believers are one in the Father and the Son, as the Father is in the Son. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 12:13, Colossians 3:14, John 17:21.
  > "In this also eminently consists our communion with the Son that we drink into the same Spirit." *[quote exact]*

## 216. Are all three, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, truly persons, given that personhood requires understanding and will?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes, all three are persons because each has understanding and will. The Father has them as the Son and Spirit are in him and proceed from him. The Son has them as he is the understanding and the Spirit is in him and proceeds from him. The Spirit has them as he is the divine will and the Son is in him.
  > "All the Three are Persons for they all have understanding and will." *[quote exact]*

## 217. Is it unreasonable to say that one divine Person has understanding or love by another Person's being in him?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): No. Scripture gives ground for this, since it teaches that the Son is the wisdom, reason and truth of God. So the Father is wise by his own wisdom, the Son, being in him. The same pattern accounts for understanding in the Spirit through the Son's being in him. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 1:24, John 1:1, John 14:6.
  > "we have Scripture ground to conclude so concerning the Father's having wisdom and understanding or reason that it is by the Son's being in Him; because we are there informed that He is the wisdom and reason and truth of God" *[quote exact]*

## 218. How can the Son be begotten and the Spirit proceed from the Father and the Son, while all three Persons are co-eternal?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards claims his scheme illustrates this. The Son is God's eternal understanding or idea of himself, and the Spirit is the eternal love flowing from Father and Son. On this account, generation and procession are relations of order within the eternal divine life, not origins in time. So all the Persons are co-eternal.
  > "Hereby we see how that it is possible for the Son to be begotten by the Father and the Holy Ghost to proceed from the Father and Son, and yet that all the Persons should be Co-etemal." *[quote exact]*

## 219. If the Father is the fountain of the Godhead, are the Son and the Holy Spirit lesser in honor than the Father?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): No. Besides the honor common to all three as God, each has a peculiar honor that is equal to the others'. The Father's honor is to be the author of infinite wisdom, while the Son's is to be that wisdom itself, from whose excellency the Father's honor as generator arises. The honor of Father and Son is to be infinitely excellent, holy and happy and the fountain of these. The Spirit's honor is equal, because he is that excellency, holiness, beauty and happiness itself.
  > "The honor of the Father and the Son is that they are infinitely excellent, or that from them infinite excellency proceeds; but the honor of the Holy Ghost is equal for He is that Divine excellency and beauty itself." *[quote exact]*

## 220. How is the Son begotten according to Edwards's psychological model of the Trinity?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): God's first knowledge is his knowledge of everything possible. Then comes a reflex act in which God views and knows himself, and so knows his own knowledge; this act is the begetting of the Son. There is in God a knowledge of knowledge, an idea of an idea, which is nothing other than the divine idea or knowledge repeated.
  > "Then comes a reflex act of knowledge and His viewing Himself and knowing Himself and so knowing His own knowledge and so the Son is begotten. There is such a thing in God as knowledge of knowledge, an idea of an idea." *[quote exact]*

## 221. In what sense was the world made for the Son of God?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): God made the world for himself from love to himself, but God loves himself only in a reflex act, by viewing himself. So God makes the world for himself as viewed and reflected on, which is God repeated or begotten in his own idea, and that is the Son. Therefore to make the world for the Godhead so understood is to make the world for the Son.
  > "For God made the world for Himself from love to Himself; but God loves Himself only in a reflex act." *[quote exact]*

## 222. Why is creation said to be made to provide a spouse for Christ?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Divine love as it flows outward is wholly directed by divine wisdom, so its objects are those whom wisdom chooses. Creation is therefore meant to gratify divine love as exercised by divine wisdom. Since Christ is divine wisdom, the world is made to gratify the love in Christ's heart, that is, to provide him an elect spouse. The other creatures exist for the sake of those elect. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 1:24.
  > "But Christ is Divine wisdom so that the world is made to gratify Divine love as exercised by Christ or to gratify the love that is in Christ's heart, or to provide a spouse for Christ." *[quote exact]*

## 223. How does redemption display the infinite worth of the Holy Spirit, if the Spirit is the delight the Father and the Son have in each other?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Redemption shows the Father's infinite excellency, because the Son prized the Father's honor so much that he stooped infinitely low rather than let it be injured. It shows the Son's infinite worth, because the Father delighted in him so much that he set aside his anger for the Son's sake. Since the Holy Spirit is the delight the Father and Son have in each other, his worth is shown to be infinite too. Edwards gives the principle that the worth of a delight is as great as the worth of the thing delighted in.
  > "So great as the worth of a thing delighted in is to any one, so great is the worth of that delight and joy itself which he has in it." *[quote exact]*

## 224. Is the Holy Spirit himself the sum of what Christ purchased for the redeemed?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards answers yes. Galatians 3:13-14 says Christ was made a curse so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit. Everything Christ purchased comes down to the Spirit: communion with God, God's favor and love, holiness (which is love to God, the Spirit dwelling in the heart), and spiritual joy, which is sharing God's joy. The blessedness of the redeemed is partaking of Christ's fullness, that is, the Spirit given to him without measure, just as the oil on the head runs down to the body (Ps. 133:2). Scripture: Galatians 3:13, Galatians 3:14, Psalms 133:2, John 3:34.
  > "The sum of all that Christ purchased for men was the Holy Ghost: (Gal. 3:13,14) "He was made a curse for us... that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith."" *[quote exact]*

## 225. Does Scripture treat 'good things' and 'the Holy Spirit' as equivalent expressions?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards argues that it does, citing Matthew 7:11, where the Father gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask. He finds the same pattern elsewhere. The spring of living water (John 4:10) and the rivers of living water (John 7:38-39) are explained as the Holy Spirit. The river of the water of life flowing from the throne of God and the Lamb (Rev. 22:1, 17) is the river of God's pleasures. So the Spirit is the sum of all spiritual good in this world and of all happiness in the next. Scripture: Matthew 7:11, John 4:10, John 7:38, John 7:39, Revelation 22:1, Revelation 22:17.
  > "Good things and the Holy Spirit are synonymous expressions in Scripture: (Matt. 7:11) "How much more shall your Heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask Him."" *[quote exact]*

## 226. Is the Holy Spirit the purchased inheritance of the saints and the main subject of the Gospel promises?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards holds that he is both. Because the portion of the Spirit the saints have now is called the earnest of the purchased inheritance (Eph. 1:14; 2 Cor. 1:22; 5:5), the Spirit must be that inheritance, of which they will have a fullness later. He is called the Spirit of promise (Eph. 1:13) and the promise of the Father (Luke 24:49). This, Edwards says, explains the force of Paul's question in Galatians 3:2 about how the Galatians received the Spirit. Scripture: Ephesians 1:13, Ephesians 1:14, 2 Corinthians 1:22, 2 Corinthians 5:5, Luke 24:49, Galatians 3:2.
  > "The Holy Ghost is the purchased possession and inheritance of the saints, as appears because that little of it which the saints have in this world is said to be the earnest of that purchased inheritance." *[quote exact]*

## 227. What created images represent the Trinity, and how do the Father, Son and Holy Spirit correspond to them?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards names two especially eminent images. The first is the human soul: the mind, its understanding or idea, and its will or affection, which Scripture calls the spirit of the mind. The second is the sun. The Father is like the sun's inner substance or constitution. The Son is like the brightness and glorious form of its disk. The Spirit is like the sun's action, its inner heat that also spreads outward to enlighten, warm and comfort the world.
  > "There is the mind, and the understanding or idea, and the spirit of the mind as it is called in Scripture, i.e., the disposition, the will or affection. The other is in the visible creation, viz., the Sun." *[quote exact]*

## 228. How can the Holy Spirit be both God's love within himself and God's self-communication to creatures?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards explains this through the sun analogy. As God's infinite love for himself and happiness in himself, the Spirit is like the sun's internal heat. As the means by which God communicates himself, the Spirit is like the sun's emanation or emitted beams. One and the same Spirit is thus both the inward love of God and its outward diffusion.
  > "The Spirit as it is God's Infinite love to Himself and happiness in Himself, is as the internal heat of the Sun, but as it is that by which God communicates Himself, it is as the emanation of the sun's action, or the emitted beams of the sun." *[quote exact]*

## 229. Do the colours of sunbeams and the rainbow in Scripture represent the Holy Spirit and his graces?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. Edwards holds that the varied rays and colours of the sun represent the love and grace of God and the graces of the Spirit. Scripture uses them this way in several places: the rainbow after the flood, the rainbow around the throne, the rainbow around Christ's head, and the silver and gold of the dove's wings, which stand for the graces of the Heavenly Dove. He takes the jewels of the breastplate, the new Jerusalem and the Temple to signify the same thing. Scripture: Ezekiel 1:28, Revelation 4:3, Revelation 10:1, Psalm 68:13, Revelation 21, Isaiah 54:11, 1 Chronicles 29:2.
  > "These beautiful colors of the sunbeams we find made use of in Scripture for this purpose, viz., to represent the graces of the Spirit" *[quote exact]*

## 230. Is the visible creation designed by God to typify spiritual realities, or is this only a human hypothesis?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Edwards holds that the whole visible creation, which is only the shadow of being, is made and ordered by God to typify and represent spiritual things. He believes the variety of the sun's colours was designed by the Creator for exactly this purpose. He offers this not as a mere hypothesis but as divine truth sufficiently ascertained by Scripture.
  > "the whole visible creation which is but the shadow of being is so made and ordered by God as to typify and represent spiritual things" *[quote exact]*
