## How should questions about the Trinity be studied and settled?

*Covers the method and starting point for inquiry into the Trinity and Christ's deity, including the priority of Scripture over reason, the role of the Church and of prayer, and whether reason can explain away the mystery. It also covers how to tell heresy from orthodoxy.*

**How the authors compare:** All the authors agree that Scripture is the primary ground for settling questions about the Trinity and that reason may not overturn what it reveals. Augustine explicitly puts scriptural authority before reasoning, Waterland prefers Scripture to the abstract reason of the thing, and Owen insists on the plain sense against figurative reinterpretation in the name of right reason. They also agree that heretical appeals to Scripture prove nothing: Athanasius treats such quotation as the devil's cloak, and Augustine lays the error on the misreaders rather than on the divine books. The authors differ in what they add to Scripture as a check or guide. Athanasius, Gregory of Nyssa, and Waterland lean heavily on the fathers: Athanasius treats novelty as a mark of heresy, Gregory holds the received tradition firm even when reasoning fails, and Waterland tests every individual reading against the consensus of the primitive Church. Edwards instead allows that Scripture with reason may say more than has usually been said, while still insisting the mystery remains. Bickersteth stresses something none of the others raise here, namely prayer, the Spirit's guidance, and moral preparation of heart before doctrinal inquiry.

Scripture: 1 Timothy 4:1-2.

### In settling a disputed Christian doctrine, should one rely chiefly on Scripture or on abstract reasoning about the nature of the thing?

*theological*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Preface; raised by the author): Chiefly on Scripture. Waterland commends Bishop Bull's method of examining Scripture more than the abstract reason of the thing. Pertinent texts should be chosen for their real sense, not their sound, and explained naturally without wresting. Texts that seem to tell against one's view must be stated at full force and answered solidly, without subtle glosses.
  > "his way was to examine carefully into Scripture, more than into the nature and reason of the thing abstractedly considered. He pitched upon such texts as were pertinent and close to the point ; did not choose them according to the sound only, but their real sense" *[quote exact]*

### Should an interpreter's reading of Scripture on a point of doctrine be tested against the consensus of the primitive Church?

*theological*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Preface; raised by the author): Yes. However forcible a person's reasonings from Scripture seem, he should not be confident of them unless the general verdict of the primitive Church also supports them. It is more likely that an individual errs in interpreting Scripture than that the universal Church erred from the beginning. Waterland presents this as the right method for explaining all doctrines.
  > "believing it easier for himself to err in interpreting Scripture, than for the universal Church to have erred from the beginning" *[quote exact]*

### If heretics defend their false opinions about the Trinity from the Scriptures, can their errors be blamed on Scripture itself?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What Augustin Requests from His Readers. The Errors of Readers Dull of Comprehension Not to Be Ascribed to the Author.; raised by the author): No. Although all heretics try to defend their false and erroneous opinions from the same Scriptures, no one can rightly ascribe their many errors to the holy testimonies of the divine books. In the same way, readers who misunderstand a careful writer on a hard and obscure subject like the Trinity err through their own failure to understand, not through the text they follow.
  > "neither can any one, in any way, rightly ascribe the numerous and various errors of heretics to the holy testimonies themselves of the divine books; although all of them endeavor to defend out of those same Scriptures their own false and erroneous opinions" *[quote exact]*

### Can the scriptural statements about the Trinity be legitimately reinterpreted figuratively, on the grounds of right reason, so as to deny that they reveal a real mystery in God?

*exegetical*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader; raised by Socinians/Unitarians): No. Scripture repeatedly and emphatically calls these things mysteries, and they fit the glorious being and wisdom of God. Those who deny the mystery in the things themselves are forced to give the scriptural words an enigmatic sense, turning almost the whole gospel into an allegory. This leaves nothing certain in anything expressed in words or writing. Right reason refuses to be used this way, so the plain and obvious sense of Scripture must be accepted.
  > "they are forced to assign such an enigmatical sense unto the words, expressions, and propositions wherein they are revealed and declared in the Scripture, as to turn almost the whole gospel into an allegory" *[quote exact]*

### If human reasoning cannot resolve a difficulty about the Trinity, should the received faith be abandoned?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. Even if our reasoning proves unequal to the problem, the tradition received by succession from the fathers must be kept firm and unmoved. We should seek from the Lord a reason that defends the faith, and if none is found, we still hold the faith unchangeably.
  > "we must keep for ever, firm and unmoved, the tradition which we received by succession from the fathers, and seek from the Lord the reason which is the advocate of our faith" *[quote exact]*

### Does Edwards's account of the Trinity explain away or remove its character as a mystery?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): No. He does not pretend to explain the Trinity fully or render it no longer a mystery; it remains the highest and deepest of divine mysteries. Yet Scripture with reason may lead us to say somewhat more of it than has usually been said, though much remains incomprehensible.
  > "I am far from pretending to explaining the Trinity so as to render it no longer a mystery. I think it to be the highest and deepest of all Divine mysteries still" *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by unnamed objector): No, it does not explain away the mystery, and no, the new difficulties are not a fatal objection. Edwards offers his account not as an explication but as a further display of what God's Word reveals; it lessens some difficulties while revealing new ones. It is no wonder that the more we are told about what is infinitely above our reach, the more visible mysteries there are, just as a well-taught divine sees more mysteries than a child yet understands more.
  > "No wonder that the more things we are told concerning that which is so infinitely above our reach, the number of visible mysteries increases." *[quote exact]*

### Does a fuller account of the Trinity drawn from Scripture make the doctrine more obscure because it multiplies the mysteries in view?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): No. It increases the number of visible mysteries only because God is seen to have told us more about Himself than was generally noticed. More knowledge brings more wonders into view and a clearer understanding at the same time. A child told a little about God sees fewer mysteries than a divinity student but understands less. Likewise the New Testament revealed more of the Trinity than the Old, which both opened God's nature further and multiplied its visible mysteries. A microscope works the same way, showing more wonders while giving truer knowledge.
  > "the things that have been observed increase the number of visible mysteries in the Godhead in no other manner than as by them we perceive that God has told us much more about it than was before generally observed" *[quote exact]*

### In what order should the doctrine of the Trinity be established: by Scripture first or by reason first?

*theological*

### Should an enquirer into the deity of Christ rely on prayer and the Holy Spirit's guidance rather than on human learning alone?

### Should a person inquiring into the doctrine of the Trinity begin with the nature of God's being, the divinity of Christ and the personality of the Holy Spirit?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 1. Introduction. Preparation of heart. Our position before God.; raised by the author): No. There is a prior inquiry: what one's own relation and position before God is, as Scripture records it. Pursued with prayerful study of God's word, this inquiry awakens by God's grace the humble disposition of mind that fits a person for the later inquiry into God's mysterious Being. Moral and spiritual preparation of heart comes before doctrinal investigation of the Godhead.
  > "I ask not then, my friends, that you should enquire first of all into the nature of God’s mysterious Being, the Divinity of Jesus Christ, and the personality of the Holy Spirit. There is a prior investigation which demands your earnest heed" *[quote exact]*

## Are the Father, Son and Holy Spirit one God or three?

*Covers the central confession that the three persons are one God of one substance, and why confessing the deity of the Son does not yield a second or lesser God or 'three Gods'.*

**How the authors compare:** All four authors conclude that Father, Son and Holy Spirit are one God, not three, while each is truly God. They reach that conclusion by different routes. Augustine rests it on the Catholic faith and the teaching of Scripture's expounders, framing it as the thesis that the Trinity is the one true God of one substance, to be shown first from Scripture and then by reasoning. Gregory of Nyssa is the most philosophical: he cites Deuteronomy's 'the Lord is one' but explicitly rejects the merely pragmatic reply of avoiding pagan polytheism, and grounds unity in the single, undifferentiated divine nature and operation, the logic of how nature-names signify (as with human nature), and the parallel of 'one Saviour'. Daniel Waterland argues polemically against Arian subordinationism, following Augustine that a greater and lesser God would make two Gods, and he distinguishes Person from Godhead so that the one Supreme God is more than one Person; Edward Henry Bickersteth instead argues from Scripture's warrant for placing in each of the Three the supreme trust due to God alone, and insists against the Unitarians that Trinitarians hold God's unity just as firmly.

### Are the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit three Gods or one God?

*theological*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.; raised by the author): One God, not three Gods. The Catholic expounders of Scripture teach that Father, Son and Holy Spirit intimate a divine unity of one and the same substance in an indivisible equality. Therefore they are not three Gods but one God, and Augustine affirms this as his own faith because it is the Catholic faith.
  > "the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit intimate a divine unity of one and the same substance in an indivisible equality; and therefore that they are not three Gods, but one God" *[quote exact]*

### If the Godhead belongs to Father, Son and Holy Spirit, must one say there are three Gods, or is there only one God?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector (Ablabius)): No. Gregory treats both alternatives as erroneous: saying 'three Gods' is unlawful, and denying the Godhead of the Son and Spirit is impious and absurd. The faith confesses three Persons without difference of nature while holding that the Godhead is one, and he sets out to show this is not inconsistent.
  > "either to say “there are three Gods,” which is unlawful, or not to acknowledge the Godhead of the Son and the Holy Spirit, which is impious and absurd." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Yes. Scripture testifies, "Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one Lord." So we must confess one God, even though the name of Godhead extends through the Holy Trinity. This follows the same principle as in human nature: the name of the nature should not be put in the plural. Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "Therefore we must confess one God, according to the testimony of Scripture, “Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one Lord,” even though the name of Godhead extends through the Holy Trinity." *[quote exact]*

### Is the Trinity the one, only and true God, with Father, Son and Holy Spirit of one and the same substance?

*theological*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > In What Manner This Work Proposes to Discourse Concerning the Trinity.; raised by the author): Yes. Augustine sets out as the thesis of his work that the Trinity is the one and only and true God. He holds that the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are rightly said, believed and understood to be of one and the same substance or essence, and he undertakes to demonstrate this from Scripture and then by reasoning.
  > "that the Trinity is the one and only and true God, and also how the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are rightly said, believed, understood, to be of one and the same substance or essence" *[quote exact]*

### If the Father is God and the Son is God, why are they not two Gods?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead. Plurality of Gods would follow only if the essence of the subjects were diverse. Since the divine nature is single and unchanging and rejects all diversity in essence, it does not admit the signification of multitude, and God is one.
  > "The Father is God: the Son is God: and yet by the same proclamation God is One, because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead." *[quote exact]*

### If the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are each, on the warrant of Scripture, objects of the supreme and undivided trust that belongs to the one infinite God alone, does it follow that all three are equally God?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Preface; raised by the author): Yes. Scripture teaches that the one infinite God claims our supreme and undivided confidence, and Scripture also warrants placing that same confidence in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost. It follows that Father, Son, and Spirit are equally God over all, the Triune Jehovah, in whom alone we trust and whom alone we adore. Scripture: Isaiah 26:4.
  > "that the same confidence is, on the warrant of Scripture, to be reposed in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Ghost; and that therefore Father, Son, and Spirit are equally God over all" *[quote exact]*

### Since salvation is confessed to come from the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, are there three Saviours, and does this show why there are not three Gods?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): There are not three Saviours. Scripture speaks of one Saviour of all men, yet no one infers that the Son does not save or that salvation comes without the Spirit. God over all saves, while the Son works salvation by the grace of the Spirit. By the same reasoning the Trinity is not called three Gods, even though the name of Godhead belongs to each. Scripture: 1 Timothy 4:10.
  > "and yet they are not on this account called in Scripture three Saviours (although salvation is confessed to proceed from the Holy Trinity): so neither are they called three Gods" *[quote exact]*

### If Christ is God at all, must he be a different individual God from the Supreme God, the Father?

*logical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do; raised by Arians (Dr. Clarke's defender (Dr. Whitby/Jackson school))): No. The objection plays on the word 'individual', taking it to mean the same individual Person, which the orthodox never claim. They hold that the Son is another Person who shares the same undivided Godhead and so is the same Supreme God. It follows that the Supreme God is more Persons than one.
  > "Who pretends that the Son is the same Person with the Father? All we assert is, that he is the same Supreme God ; that is, partaker of the same undivided Godhead." *[quote exact]*

*theological*

That is why he objects that the name 'Unitarian' misstates the real point at issue between the two parties.

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No, that explanation is inadequate. It might satisfy guileless people, but it gives no real solution to those who demand that we either deny the Godhead of the Three Persons or call them three Gods. A fuller reply grounded in the truth about the divine nature and how names signify is required.
  > "in the case of the others who require that one of the alternatives they propose should be established" *[quote exact]*

## Is the Son truly God by nature?

*Presents scriptural proofs that the Son is truly and naturally God, equal with the Father. It answers the views that he is god only by participation or by office, and asks what a genuine confession of his deity requires.*

**How the authors compare:** Augustine, Waterland, Athanasius and Bickersteth all conclude that the Son is truly and properly God, not God in a lesser, created or merely titular sense. They support this in different ways. Augustine argues most directly from Scripture's positive statements, reading John 1:1 with 1 John 5:20 and Philippians 2:6 to show that the Son is very God and equal with the Father. Waterland argues mainly by answering objections: Isaiah's monotheism excludes inferior as well as coordinate gods, distinct naming of the Father shows only distinct Persons, and 1 Corinthians 8:6 sets Father and Son together against idols. He also makes a linguistic point, that speaking of 'Christ's Divinity' while meaning a relative godhood is equivocation. Athanasius stresses the incoherence of calling Christ God while ranking him a creature, and he distinguishes the Son, who is one in essence with the Father and is participated in, from saints who are 'gods' only by participating in him. Bickersteth adds a narrower caution: Unitarians may use scriptural language about Christ while regarding him as an exalted creature, so using such phrases is not the same as confessing his Godhead.

### Does John 1:1 show that the Son (the Word) is truly God and not merely called God?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. Not Only the Father, But the Trinity, is Affirmed to Be Immortal. All Things are Not from the Father Alone, But Also from the Son. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.; raised by the author): Yes. John says the Word was with God and the Word was God, and the Word is the only Son who was later made flesh. Combined with 1 John 5:20, which calls the Son 'the true God, and eternal life,' Scripture shows he is not only God but very God. Scripture: John 1:1, John 1:14, 1 John 5:20.
  > "This is the true God, and eternal life." *[quote exact]*

### Does Scripture explicitly call the Son equal with God the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. Not Only the Father, But the Trinity, is Affirmed to Be Immortal. All Things are Not from the Father Alone, But Also from the Son. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.; raised by the author): Yes. In Philippians 2:6 the apostle expressly says that the Son, being in the form of God, "thought it not robbery to be equal with God." Here "God" is used specially of the Father, as it is in "the head of Christ is God." Scripture: Philippians 2:6, 1 Corinthians 11:3.
  > "the apostle has not refrained from using the very word itself, but has said most expressly, “Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God;”" *[quote exact]*

### Can the Son be called God in John 1:1 in the sense of an inferior, created God distinct from the one Supreme God?

*theological*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do; raised by Arians (Dr. Clarke's defender)): No. The texts of Isaiah are purposely designed to exclude all inferior as well as coordinate gods, for example Isaiah 43:10, 'Before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me.' So the Son must either be entirely excluded from deity or not at all. If he is not excluded, he is comprehended in the one Supreme God and is one with him. Scripture: John 1:1, Hebrews 1:8, Romans 9:5, Isaiah 43:10.
  > "he must either be entirely excluded, or not at all: and if he be not excluded, he is comprehended in the one Supreme God, and is one with him" *[quote exact]*

### Since the Father is named in distinction from the Son in texts about the one God, does it follow that the Son is not the one God?

*logical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do; raised by Arians (Dr. Clarke's defender)): No. The distinct naming proves only that the Persons are distinct, which tells against the Sabellians, who make one Person of two. It says nothing against the Son's deity. To infer that, the objector would need to prove that no sufficient reason can be given for styling the Father the only God unless the Son is excluded.
  > "unless you can prove this negative proposition, that no sufficient reason can be assigned for styling the Father the only God, without supposing that the Son is excluded." *[quote exact]*

## How is the Son eternally begotten of the Father?

*Covers the manner of the Son's eternal generation, including the psychological analogy of God's perfect idea of himself and the Son as the Father's own Word and Wisdom. It also asks why God does not generate himself and how begetting and procession fit with co-eternity.*

**How the authors compare:** Most answers here come from Jonathan Edwards. He holds that the Son is begotten as God's perfect, substantial idea of Himself. God must become His own object in order to delight in Himself. His reflex knowledge, an idea of an idea, repeats the divine essence, so the Son is a distinct person yet the very same God, and co-eternal because God never lacks self-understanding. Edwards grounds this in philosophical reasoning about reflection, an analogy of a man with a perfect self-idea being 'twice at once,' and Scripture texts calling Christ the image, form, and wisdom of God (including Proverbs 8). Athanasius agrees that the Son is the Father's very and only Word and Wisdom, but he presses the point polemically against making the Son a mere partaker of some other wisdom, arguing from what befits one who 'was God' rather than from a model of divine self-knowledge. Augustine addresses a different question: he denies that God generated Himself, since nothing generates its own existence, and so excludes self-generation without offering any positive account of how the begetting occurs.

### How can the Son be begotten of the Father and the Spirit proceed from Father and Son, while all three are co-eternal?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): On Edwards's model the Son is God's eternal idea or understanding of Himself and the Spirit is the eternal love between Father and Son; since God never lacks understanding or love, the begetting and procession involve no temporal priority, so all are co-eternal and the Spirit proceeds from both.
  > "Hereby we see how that it is possible for the Son to be begotten by the Father and the Holy Ghost to proceed from the Father and Son, and yet that all the Persons should be Co-etemal." *[quote exact]*

### Is the idea God has of Himself truly God, so that the Son is begotten by God's self-understanding?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. God understands Himself with perfect clearness, fullness and strength. His essence is wholly substance and wholly act, so His idea of Himself is absolutely Himself, the Divine nature and essence again. By God's thinking of the Deity, another person is begotten who is the same God and the very same Divine nature.
  > "that idea which God hath of Himself is absolutely Himself. This representation of the Divine nature and essence is the Divine nature and essence again: so that by God's thinking of the Deity must certainly be generated." *[quote exact]*

### Must God's knowledge or view of Himself be distinct from His mere direct existence?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. If God beholds Himself so as to delight and rejoice in Himself, He must become His own object. There must therefore be a duplicity: God and the idea of God, something answering to our reflection though without its imperfection.
  > "However, if God beholds Himself so as thence to have delight and joy in Himself He must become his own object. There must be a duplicity. There is God and the idea of God" *[quote exact]*

### Is the Son of God, as God's begotten idea of Himself, a distinct person yet the same God as the Father?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. *philosophical*

## How are the persons distinct without dividing the one God?

*Explains how the persons differ by relation and mode of subsistence while sharing one nature, intellect and will. It also covers why 'unbegotten' and 'begotten' do not mean different natures and why sharing one nature does not confuse the persons.*

**How the authors compare:** Augustine, Gregory of Nyssa and Jonathan Edwards agree that the persons are really distinct while the divine nature or substance remains one, and that the distinction lies in the relations of origin rather than in any difference of nature. Augustine grounds it simply in begetting: the one who begets is not the one begotten. Gregory develops a more technical account. Persons are told apart by peculiar attributes and proper names, as with Luke and Stephen, and specifically by cause and caused. Terms like 'unbegotten' describe how a person exists, not what he is, which Gregory illustrates with the analogy of a husbandman's tree. He also distinguishes the Spirit from the Son by having the Spirit come from the Father through the Son. Edwards argues instead from a psychological model: the Son is God's idea of himself and the Spirit is God's love and delight. On this basis he grants each person understanding and will while insisting there is only one understanding, the Son, which indwells the Father and the Spirit. On divine action, Gregory and Augustine both teach one indivisible operation, though with different emphases. Gregory stresses its ordered movement from the Father, through the Son, perfected in the Spirit. Augustine pairs indivisible working with manifestations proper to a single person, such as the Son's birth and the Father's voice.

### If Father, Son and Holy Spirit are one substance, does that mean the Father is the same person as the Son?

*logical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.; raised by the author): No. The Father has begotten the Son, so he who is the Father is not the Son. The Son is begotten by the Father, so he who is the Son is not the Father. The unity of substance does not erase the distinction that comes from the relation of begetting.
  > "although the Father hath begotten the Son, and so He who is the Father is not the Son; and the Son is begotten by the Father, and so He who is the Son is not the Father" *[quote exact]*

### How are the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit each to be understood as modes of the Deity subsisting?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): The Father is the Deity subsisting in the prime, unoriginated and most absolute manner, the Deity in its direct existence. The Son is the Deity generated by God's understanding, God's idea of himself in which the Deity subsists. The Holy Spirit is the Deity subsisting in act, the divine essence flowing out and breathed forth in God's infinite love to and delight in himself. The whole divine essence truly and distinctly subsists in both the divine idea and the divine love, so each is a properly distinct Person.
  > "The Son is the Deity generated by God's understanding, or having an idea of Himself and subsisting in that idea. The Holy Ghost is the Deity subsisting in act, or the Divine essence flowing out and breathed forth in God's Infinite love to and delight in Himself." *[quote exact]*

### How is the Holy Spirit distinguished from the Son if both are from the Father?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): The Son is directly from the first Cause, while the Spirit is from the Father by means of the Son. This preserves the Son's attribute of being Only-begotten. Yet the Son's mediation does not shut out the Spirit from His natural relation to the Father.
  > "For one is directly from the first Cause, and another by that which is directly from the first Cause; so that the attribute of being Only-begotten abides without doubt in the Son" *[quote exact]*

### Are the Son and the Holy Spirit equal in honor to the Father despite proceeding from Him?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. Besides the common honor of all being God, each has an equal peculiar honor: the Father is the author and fountain of wisdom, excellency and happiness; the Son is that Divine wisdom itself, whose excellency gives the generating its honor; the Spirit is the Divine excellency, holiness, beauty and happiness itself.
  > "The honor of the Father and the Son is that they are infinitely excellent, or that from them infinite excellency proceeds; but the honor of the Holy Ghost is equal for He is that Divine excellency and beauty itself." *[quote exact]*

### Do the Father, Son and Holy Spirit work separately or as one indivisible operation, and how does each take part in it?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Gregory of Nyssa and Augustine agree that the three Persons do not act separately but work indivisibly. Gregory grounds this in the order of a single operation that issues from the Father, proceeds through the Son and is perfected in the Spirit, so it is never three actions. Augustine grounds it in the Persons' indivisibility of being, while also allowing that certain manifestations, such as the Son's birth and the Father's voice, belong to one Person alone.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Every operation from God to creation originates from the Father, proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit. It is one motion and disposition of good will, communicated from the Father through the Son to the Spirit. It is not three separate actions matching the number of the Persons.
  > "every operation which extends from God to the Creation, and is named according to our variable conceptions of it, has its origin from the Father, and proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. All providence, care and superintendence is one operation, not three, directed by the Trinity but not divided according to the number of Persons. It issues from the Father as from a spring, is brought into operation by the Son, and is perfected by the power of the Spirit, so that no act is the work of one Person in isolation. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 12:11.
  > "being, indeed, directed by the Holy Trinity, yet not severed by a threefold division according to the number of the Persons contemplated in the Faith, so that each of the acts, contemplated by itself, should be the work of the Father alone" *[quote exact]*
- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.; raised by the author): Indivisibly. Just as the three Persons are indivisible in being, so they work indivisibly. Augustine affirms this together with the fact that certain manifestations belong to one Person alone, such as the Son's birth and the Father's voice.
  > "although the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, as they are indivisible, so work indivisibly" *[quote exact]*

### Are all three members of the Trinity truly Persons, each having understanding and will?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. All three are Persons because each has understanding and will: the Father as the Son and Spirit are in Him and proceed from Him; the Son as He is the understanding and the Spirit is in Him and proceeds from Him; the Spirit as He is the Divine will and the Son is in Him.
  > "All the Three are Persons for they all have understanding and will. There is understanding and will in the Father, as the Son and the Holy Ghost are in Him and proceed from Him." *[quote exact]*

### Do the three Persons of the Trinity have three distinct understandings, or one and the same understanding?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): No. Theologians have not been accustomed to suppose three understandings in the Godhead, but one and the same understanding. That one understanding is the Son, and the Father and the Spirit understand because the Son indwells them.
  > "divines have not been wont to suppose that these three had three distinct understandings, but all one and the same understanding." *[quote exact]*

### Does denying any difference of nature among Father, Son, and Spirit confuse or mix the Persons?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector): No. While confessing the invariable character of the nature, we affirm a difference in respect of cause and what is caused. This difference alone distinguishes one Person from another: one is the Cause and another is of the Cause. Therefore community of nature does not confound the Persons.
  > "while we confess the invariable character of the nature, we do not deny the difference in respect of cause, and that which is caused, by which alone we apprehend that one Person is distinguished from another" *[quote exact]*

### Do the terms 'cause' and 'caused,' or 'unbegotten' and 'begotten,' denote different natures in the Father and the Son?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. They indicate only the difference in manner of existence, not the nature. The question of what something is differs from the question of how it exists. A husbandman who says whether a tree was planted or grew of itself says nothing of its nature. Likewise 'unbegotten' tells us how the Father exists, not what He is.
  > "when we learn that He is unbegotten, we are taught in what mode He exists, and how it is fit that we should conceive Him as existing, but what He is we do not hear in that phrase." *[quote exact]*

### What distinguishes individual persons who share one nature, such as Luke and Stephen?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Persons are distinguished by the peculiar attributes considered in each, and are signified by proper names rather than by the name of the nature. Luke is a man and Stephen is a man, but being a man does not make one Luke or Stephen. Persons can therefore be counted, while the nature remains one.
  > "the idea of the persons admits of that separation which is made by the peculiar attributes considered in each severally, and when they are combined is presented to us by means of number" *[quote exact]*

## Is the Holy Spirit fully God?

*Shows that the Holy Spirit is very God, equal with the Father and the Son. The evidence covered includes his worship, his indwelling of believers as a temple, his knowledge of hidden things and his role as the inheritance Christ purchased.*

**How the authors compare:** Augustine, Gregory of Nyssa and Jonathan Edwards all affirm that the Holy Spirit is fully God, equal with the Father and the Son, but they reach this by different routes. Augustine argues by elimination and from Scripture: whatever is not a creature is very God, and the Spirit cannot be a creature because Scripture shows the saints rendering him latreia, the service due to God alone, and makes believers' bodies, which are Christ's members, his temple. Gregory of Nyssa draws on a single scriptural episode, Ananias and Sapphira, to show that the Spirit has the divine power of seeing hidden things. Edwards argues from the economy of redemption and his own account of the Spirit as the mutual love and delight of the Father and the Son: the Spirit is himself the thing Christ purchased, the inheritance and promise of the Gospel, and so is equal to the infinite price paid and due equal glory. Edwards differs in emphasis and explicitly sets his view against a traditional view on which the Spirit merely applies benefits purchased by Christ, a role that on its own would make his part seem small. Augustine and Gregory focus instead on divine attributes and the worship owed to the Spirit.

### If the Holy Spirit is not a creature, does it follow that he is very God, equal and consubstantial with the Father and the Son?

*logical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.; raised by the author): Yes. Men too are called gods, so being called "god" alone is not enough. But what is not a creature is very God. The Spirit is therefore absolutely equal with the Father and the Son, consubstantial and co-eternal in the unity of the Trinity. Scripture: Psalm 82:6.
  > "But if not a creature, then not only God (for men likewise are called gods), but also very God; and therefore absolutely equal with the Father and the Son, and in the unity of the Trinity consubstantial and co-eternal." *[quote exact]*

### How is the Holy Spirit distinguished from the Father and the Son, and is he equal to them?

*theological*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.; raised by the author): The Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son but the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. He is himself co-equal with the Father and the Son and belongs to the unity of the Trinity.
  > "the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son, but only the Spirit of the Father and of the Son, Himself also co-equal with the Father and the Son, and pertaining to the unity of the Trinity" *[quote exact]*

### Is the Holy Spirit's role in the work of redemption equal to that of the Father and the Son, or does he merely apply the benefits purchased by Christ?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. The Father appoints and provides the Redeemer, accepts the price and grants the thing purchased. The Son is the Redeemer and is himself the price. The Holy Ghost communicates the thing purchased by communicating Himself, and He is the thing purchased. Since the price and the thing bought with it are equal, the Spirit's share is not a lesser, merely subservient application of benefits.
  > "The Father appoints and provides the Redeemer, and Himself accepts the price and grants the thing purchased; the Son is the Redeemer by offering Himself and is the price; and the Holy Ghost immediately communicates to us the thing purchased by communicating Himself, and He is the thing purchased." *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Not on the traditional view, but yes on Edwards's view. If the Spirit only hands to us the blessing after it is purchased, subservient to the other two, his part would be little compared to the Father giving his Son and the Son paying an infinite price. But if the Spirit is himself the love of God and the very thing purchased, there is an equality: the thing bought is equal to the price, and the glory of affording it is the glory of the thing's worth.
  > "But according to this there is an equality. To be the love of God to the world is as much as for the Father and the Son to do so much from love to the world, and to be the thing purchased was as much as to be the price." *[quote exact]*

### Is the Holy Spirit due equal glory with the Father and the Son for the love shown in the work of redemption?

*theological*

## Can human beings know God's nature, and how should we speak of it?

*Covers God's incomprehensibility, whether appeals to it hide ignorance, and the limits of ideas drawn from bodies and minds. It also covers anthropomorphic language in Scripture, how an unchangeable God creates temporal things, and how minds are brought to see the triune God.*

**How the authors compare:** Bickersteth and Augustine agree that real knowledge of God is attainable but never exhaustive. Bickersteth grounds this in Isaiah 45:15 and 45:19, promising all necessary and saving knowledge to the humble and contrite. Augustine gives a moral and epistemological account: the mind's weak eye is dazzled by God's light and must be purified and strengthened by the righteousness of faith, and the difficulty lies in the inquirer, not in the truth or in evasive teachers. He also warns against attributing bodily qualities or mental changes to the unchangeable God, explaining that Scripture's bodily and emotional language accommodates 'babes' so that understanding may rise by degrees. Athanasius instead takes a Christological line, holding that rightly contemplating creation leads to the Word through whom it was made and through him to the Father; his two answers are labeled 'Yes' and 'No' but give the same reasoning, the 'No' answering whether creation can make God known apart from the Son, and both cite Christ's words to Philip.

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 1. Introduction. Preparation of heart. Our position before God.; raised by the author): Yes, though not exhaustively. God's self-revelation must contain mysteries beyond the reach of finite minds, as Isaiah 45:15 shows. Yet God has also said that he did not tell the seed of Jacob to seek him in vain (Isaiah 45:19). So all necessary and saving knowledge is promised to the humble student, because the Lord has respect to the lowly and is near to the contrite. Scripture: Isaiah 45:15, Isaiah 45:19, Psalm 138:6, Psalm 34:18.
  > "Thus though there will, there must be in the self-revelation of Him, whose ways are past finding out, mysteries beyond the reach and range of our finite capacities, all necessary and saving knowledge is promised to the humble student" *[quote exact]*

## What do divine names and attributes reveal about God's essence?

*Covers the meaning of names such as 'God', 'Godhead' and 'I am that I am', and what attributes like 'incorruptible' reveal about God. It also asks whether such names belong to all three persons and in what sense everything in God is God.*

**How the authors compare:** Gregory of Nyssa supplies most of the answers here, and he takes a consistently apophatic line: no divine name, including "Godhead," signifies or comprehends the divine essence. He argues this from Scripture, which treats the nature as unnameable, and from the reasoning that the infinite cannot be bounded even by a name. On his account names either mark what God is not ("incorruptible") or what God does ("Giver of life"), and "God" itself derives from the operation of beholding, an operation Scripture ascribes equally to Father, Son, and Spirit. Augustine sounds a different note: he reads "I am that I am" as positively signifying a unique, proper mode of being belonging to God alone and to no creature, so for him at least this one name points to what is distinctive of God, not merely to an operation or a negation. Jonathan Edwards addresses a separate question, using philosophical reasoning rather than scriptural citation. He limits the maxim "everything in God is God" to God's real attributes, understanding and love, in which the Deity subsists as distinct Persons, and denies that it applies to mere modalities like immutability or omnipresence. This ties certain attributes directly to the Trinitarian Persons in a way Gregory, who makes attributes non-essential descriptions, does not.

### Does the operation of beholding, from which the name "God" is derived, belong to only one Person of the Trinity or to all three?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): It extends to all three Persons, so none can be excluded from the name "God." Scripture attributes seeing equally to the Father ("See, O God our defender"), to the Son ("Jesus, seeing their thoughts"), and to the Holy Spirit, who detected Ananias' secret sin and gave Peter the power to see it. Scripture: Psalm 84:9, Matthew 9:4.
  > "no one of the Persons in the Trinity could reasonably be excluded from such an appellation on the ground of the sense involved in the word. For Scripture attributes the act of seeing equally to Father, Son, and Holy Spirit." *[quote exact]*

### From what is the name "Godhead" (θεότης) derived, and what does it signify?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): It is derived from an operation, namely θέα, beholding. Because we name God from His operations we know, and we know His operation of surveying and overseeing all things, even our thoughts and invisible things, He who is our beholder (θεατής) is called θεός, God.
  > "we suppose that Godhead, or θεότης, is so called from θέα, or beholding, and that He who is our θεατής or beholder, by customary use and by the instruction of the Scriptures, is called θεός, or God" *[quote exact]*

### Does the word "Godhead" (or any other divine name) signify or comprehend the very essence of the divine nature?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector ("most men" who take Godhead as a proper name of the nature)): No. Following Scripture, the divine nature is unnameable and unspeakable; every term, whether invented by human custom or handed down in Scripture, explains our conceptions of the divine nature but does not include the signification of that nature itself. Each term applied to God has its own distinct sense that makes known something about the nature, not what it is in essence.
  > "every term either invented by the custom of men, or handed down to us by the Scriptures, is indeed explanatory of our conceptions of the Divine Nature, but does not include the signification of that nature itself" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector (those who deny that Godhead is to be conceived as an operation)): No. The divine nature is infinite and incomprehensible in every respect, and what is absolutely unlimited cannot be limited even by a name. The Deity is above every name, and since 'Godhead' is a name, it cannot express the nature itself. Gregory therefore takes it to signify an operation. Scripture: Philippians 2:9.
  > "we say that the Deity is above every name: and “Godhead” is a name. Now it cannot be that the same thing should at once be a name and be accounted as above every name." *[quote exact]*

### What does God's name "I am that I am" signify, given that bodies and souls also are said to be?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > This Work is Written Against Those Who Sophistically Assail the Faith of the Trinity, Through Misuse of Reason. They Who Dispute Concerning God Err from a Threefold Cause. Holy Scripture, Removing What is False, Leads Us on by Degrees to Things Divine. What True Immortality is. We are Nourished by Faith, that We May Be Enabled to Apprehend Things Divine.; raised by the author): It signifies being in a special sense proper to God alone and found in no creature. Since body and soul are also said in some sense to be, Scripture would not so express itself unless it meant to be understood in a unique sense belonging to God. Scripture: Exodus 3:14.
  > "For since both body and soul also are said in some sense to be, Holy Scripture certainly would not so express itself unless it meant to be understood in some special sense of the term." *[quote exact]*

### In what sense is the theologians' maxim that 'everything in God is God' true?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): It holds of God's real attributes, not of mere modalities. It makes no rational sense to say God's immutability, omnipresence, or right of government is God. It is true of God's understanding and love, because the Deity subsists distinctly in them, which makes them distinct divine Persons.
  > "But if it be meant that the real attributes of God, viz., His understanding and love are God, then what we have said may in some measure explain how it is so, for Deity subsists in them distinctly; so they are distinct Divine Persons." *[quote exact]*

### What do divine attributes such as "incorruptible" or "Giver of life" actually tell us about God?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): They either forbid us to conceive what we ought not of God or teach us what we ought to conceive, but they do not explain the nature itself. "Incorruptible" declares what His nature does not suffer, not what it is that does not suffer corruption; "Giver of life" shows what He gives, not what the giver is.
  > "when we say that He is incorruptible, we declare what His nature does not suffer, but we do not express what that is which does not suffer corruption" *[quote exact]*

## Do texts and arguments that seem to subordinate the Son undermine his equality with the Father?

*Covers passages that appear to place the Son below the Father, Novatian's comments, the Son's suffering in weakness, and Arian claims that the Son lacks knowledge of the Father and is mutable.*

**How the authors compare:** Waterland, Augustine and Athanasius all conclude that texts and statements which seem to subordinate the Son do not undermine his equality with the Father, but they reach this by different routes. Waterland relies chiefly on a priority of order, or origin. In his reading, Novatian's remarks deny only that the Son is unbegotten or first in order, and that distinction is what keeps Father and Son from being two Gods. He extends this priority of the Father as first of the Three into a general key for subordinating texts that concern Christ in his higher nature. Augustine instead explains Christ's crucified weakness by the two ways Scripture speaks of him: his divine power, by which he is equal to the Father, and his human infirmity. Waterland likewise refers some texts to Christ as Man or Mediator, but he does not make that the whole answer. Athanasius takes a more polemical line against the Thalia's claims of alterability and deficient knowledge. He rejects them as blasphemy on the ground that the Son is the very Image of the Father who has seen him, rather than explaining them by order or by Christ's two natures.

### Does Novatian's remark on Philippians 2:6, that the Son 'never compared himself with God the Father', deny that the Son is equal to the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do; raised by Arians (Waterland's anonymous opponent, citing Novatian and Dr. Clarke)): No. Novatian gives the reason: the Son remembered that he was from his Father, that is, begotten and not unbegotten. The Son never claimed equality with the Father in respect of origin. He knew himself to be second in order, not the first Person of the Trinity. Hilary and Phoebadius use similar language and cannot be suspected of Arianism. Scripture: Philippians 2:6.
  > "He never pretended to an equality with the Father, in respect of his origina/, knowing himself to be second only in order, not the first Person of the ever blessed Trinity." *[quote exact]*

### Does Novatian's statement that 'two found equal would have made two Gods' count against the full coequality of the Son with the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do; raised by Arians (Waterland's anonymous opponent, following Dr. Clarke)): No. Read in full, it means only that if both were equal in origin, both unbegotten, there would be two Gods. Father and Son are not two Gods because they are not both unoriginated. This was the common Catholic answer to the charge of tritheism, both before and after Nicaea. Rightly understood, the passage is strong testimony for the coequality the orthodox defend; Whitby once read it as showing Christ equal to the Father in every respect except being God of God.
  > "There is nothing more in it than this, that Father and Son are not two Gods, because they are not both uwnoriqinated: which is the common answer made by the Catholics to the charge of Tritheism ;" *[quote exact]*

*theological*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > This Work is Written Against Those Who Sophistically Assail the Faith of the Trinity, Through Misuse of Reason. They Who Dispute Concerning God Err from a Threefold Cause. Holy Scripture, Removing What is False, Leads Us on by Degrees to Things Divine. What True Immortality is. We are Nourished by Faith, that We May Be Enabled to Apprehend Things Divine.; raised by the author): Yes. Christ has a divine power by which he is equal to the Father, and he also has a human infirmity by which he was crucified. Scripture speaks of him in two ways corresponding to these, so his weakness in the flesh does not lessen his equality with the Father. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 2:2.
  > "not by that divine virtue wherein He is equal to the Father, but by that human infirmity whereby He was crucified." *[quote exact]*

### How should texts that seem to subordinate the Son to the Father be explained consistently with the Son's full divinity?

*theological*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do; raised by Arians (Dr. Clarke's defender)): Some refer to Christ as Man or as Mediator. Those that certainly concern him in a higher capacity are explained by the principle, held with the ancients, that a priority of order belongs to the Father as the first of the blessed Three.   > "some are meant of Christ as Man, or as Mediator: and those which certainly respect him in a higher capacity, may be accounted for on this principle, that we reserve, with the ancients, a priority of order to the Father" *[quote exact]*

### Does the Son lack exact knowledge of the Father, and is he alterable in nature like the creatures?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > The Importance of the Subject. ## How important is the doctrine of the Trinity and Christ's deity for salvation?

*Covers whether the Trinity and the Godhead of Christ bear directly on salvation, and what follows from denying the Son's true deity.*

**How the authors compare:** Athanasius, John Owen and Edward Henry Bickersteth all hold that the doctrine of the Son's deity, and with it the Trinity, is no peripheral matter but bears directly on salvation and the integrity of the whole faith. They argue for it differently. Athanasius gives a theological chain argument: because the Son reveals the Father, supplies the Spirit, is first-begotten from the dead and is truly generated from the Father, error about the Word corrupts belief about the Father, the Spirit, the Resurrection and the Incarnation. Owen argues that the Trinity and the person of Christ stand or fall together, citing Cerinthus as a historical example, and he notes that both sides of the controversy admit its saving importance; he alone draws the lesson that these truths call for reverent, humble adoration of God's incomprehensible nature. Bickersteth puts the most stress on the individual's eternal destiny, grounding it in Scripture's linking of life with believing the Son and abiding in the doctrine of Christ, and calls Christ's deity the foundation truth on which eternal life or death turns.

*theological*

Whoever is ignorant of the Son's true generation from the Father must also go wrong about his incarnate presence. Scripture: Colossians 1:18, Matthew 11:27.
  > "For how can he speak truth concerning the Father, who denies the Son, that reveals concerning Him? or how can he be orthodox concerning the Spirit, while he speaks profanely of the Word that supplies the Spirit?" *[quote exact]*

### Is the doctrine of the Trinity an ordinary point of religious controversy, or does it directly concern salvation?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader; raised by the author): It directly concerns salvation and is no ordinary controversy.   > "They are things which immediately and directly in themselves concern the eternal salvation of the souls of men, and their consideration ought always to be attended with a due sense of their weight and importance." *[quote exact]*

### Are the doctrine of the Trinity and the doctrine of the person (Deity) of Christ logically bound together, so that denying one entails denying the other?

*theological*

This is why Cerinthus's attack on Christ's person was at the same time an attack on the holy Trinity.

*theological*

## Which scriptural texts reveal the plurality of persons in God?

*Covers early indications of plurality in Genesis, the baptismal formula of Matthew 28:19, the benediction of 2 Corinthians 13:14 and the doxology of Romans 11:36.*

**How the authors compare:** Only on Romans 11:36 do two authors answer the same question. Augustine and Jonathan Edwards both read it of Father, Son and Holy Spirit, assigning "of Him" to the Father, "through Him" to the Son and "in Him" to the Spirit. Their grounds differ. Augustine argues from the grammar and context of the text: the singular "to whom be glory" and the opening reference to the wisdom "of God" show the three to be one God. Edwards instead reads the verse through redemption: the Spirit is himself all our good, so God is the one of whom good is purchased, the purchaser, and the thing purchased. The same pneumatological emphasis governs Edwards's reading of 2 Corinthians 13:14, where he holds that grace, love and communion are one benefit, the Spirit. John Owen, by contrast, locates the first revelation of plurality in the Son's divine personality, as shown by the Jews' opposition to Christ. He treats the baptismal formula as the church's foundational confession of the whole doctrine, an ecclesial and confessional emphasis that the others do not address.

### Where is the first discovery of a plurality of persons in the divine essence found?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (The Preface; raised by the author): It lies in the revelation of the divine nature and personality of the Son. For this reason, the Jews who opposed and persecuted Christ for declaring himself God are rightly regarded as the first assertors of the anti-Trinitarian misbelief.
  > "the first discovery of a plurality of persons in the divine essence consists in the revelation of the divine nature and personality of the Son" *[quote exact]*

### Is the baptismal formula "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" an expression of the doctrine of the Trinity?

*exegetical*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (The Preface; raised by the author): Yes. By Christ's special institution the church is initiated into the express profession of the Trinity through baptism in the threefold name. This confession comprises the whole truth contended for. Because it is placed at the very entrance into all obedience to Christ, it is made the doctrinal foundation of the church. Scripture: Matthew 28:19.
  > "which confession comprises the whole of the truth contended for, and by the indispensable placing of it at the first entrance into all obedience unto him, is made the doctrinal foundation of the church" *[quote exact]*

*exegetical*

  > "They are not different benefits but the same that the Apostle here wisheth, viz., the Holy Ghost: in partaking of the Holy Ghost, we possess and enjoy the love and grace of the Father and the Son, for the Holy Ghost is that love and grace" *[quote exact]*

## Do texts calling the Father the 'only' God exclude the Son?

*Covers exclusive monotheistic texts such as John 17:3, Ephesians 4:6, Isaiah's 'no God besides me', Psalm 72:18 and the 'God of Abraham' passages. It explains how they exclude creatures and false gods rather than the Son.*

**How the authors compare:** Augustine and Waterland agree that exclusive language applied to the Father ("only," "one God," "none besides me") does not exclude the Son, and that such texts are best read of the one God who includes the Son (and, in Augustine and in Waterland's reading of Ephesians 4:6, the whole Trinity). Augustine argues from Scripture's own usage: the Son as Wisdom says "I alone" without excluding the Father, and the Son does whatever the Father does, including raising the dead, so "only" means Father, Son and Holy Spirit together. Waterland relies more on patristic precedent (Novatian and "the ancients"), on reading "only" as set against idols, and on the Father being "only God" primarily, as Fountain of the Trinity, rather than exclusively. Above all he relies on a reductio against the Arian scheme and Clarke: since the Isaiah texts and the first Commandment exclude every other god absolutely, not just rival supreme gods, a Son outside the one God would be no God and worshipping him would be idolatry. His divergent "Yes" and "No" answers on the Isaiah texts are not a real disagreement: the "Yes" answers state what would follow if the Son were excluded, and every answer concludes that Christ is included in the one Supreme God as a distinct Person.

## Do the Father, Son and Holy Spirit act as one in their works?

*Covers the persons' undivided operation in giving life, judging, speaking from heaven and acting through the Spirit. It also asks whether there is any time-sequence among the persons and whether only the Son became incarnate and suffered.*

**How the authors compare:** Gregory of Nyssa and Augustine both hold that the Father, Son and Holy Spirit act inseparably, but their answers address different sides of the question. Gregory argues for the unity of the divine action: one life is given, not three; the Father judges through the Son; the Son works by the Spirit; and the movement of the divine will from Father through Son to Spirit involves no delay. From this he concludes that we do not speak of three Givers of life or use the plural for any divine attribute. Augustine, while affirming that the three Persons work indivisibly, stresses that certain events belong to one Person alone. The Son alone was born, crucified, buried, raised and ascended, and the heavenly voice at the baptism and Transfiguration was the Father's alone. Gregory's emphasis falls on the single, ordered operation of the Trinity, which he grounds in Scriptural texts about judgment and the Spirit; Augustine's falls on the distinctness of the Persons within that inseparable working, which he grounds in the Catholic faith and the Gospel narratives.

### Since Scripture says the gift of life comes from the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, do we receive three lives or three Givers of life?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. Though there are three Persons and three names, one and the same life is wrought in us. It is wrought by the Father, prepared by the Son, and depends on the will of the Spirit. So we do not speak of three Givers of life, nor of three Good beings, nor use the plural for any other divine attribute.
  > "we do not consider that we have had bestowed upon us three lives, one from each Person separately; but the same life is wrought in us by the Father, and prepared by the Son, and depends on the will of the Holy Spirit." *[quote exact]*

### Does Scripture contradict itself when it says God judges all the earth and also that the Father judges no man?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. The Father judges all the earth through the Son, to whom He has committed all judgment. Everything the Son does refers back to the Father, and all the Son's judgment conforms to the Father's will. So the Father is at once Judge of all and judges no man. There are not two judges, and neither Person is excluded from the authority to judge. Scripture: Genesis 18:25, John 5:22.
  > "He Who judges all the earth does this by His Son to Whom He has committed all judgment; and everything which is done by the Only-begotten has its reference to the Father" *[quote exact]*

### Does the Son act through the Holy Spirit, as shown by Isaiah's 'Spirit of judgment' and Christ's casting out devils 'by the Spirit of God'?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Yes. The Father exercises the power of superintendence through the Only-begotten, and the Son perfects every power by the Holy Spirit. He judges by the Spirit of judgment and casts out devils by the Spirit of God. That single instance stands for every form of doing good, because the Persons' action is one. Scripture: Isaiah 4:4, Matthew 12:28, 1 Corinthians 1:24.
  > "where He includes every form of doing good in a partial description, by reason of the unity of action: for the name derived from operation cannot be divided among many where the result of their mutual operation is one." *[quote exact]*

### Is there any temporal delay or succession in the divine action as it moves from the Father through the Son to the Holy Spirit?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. The motion of the divine will from the Father, through the Son, to the Spirit has no delay, either existent or conceived. Good things are brought to perfection without mark of time or distinction.
  > "there is no delay, existent or conceived, in the motion of the Divine will from the Father, through the Son, to the Spirit" *[quote exact]*

*theological*

  > "Yet not that this Trinity was born of the Virgin Mary, and crucified under Pontius Pilate, and buried, and rose again the third day, and ascended into heaven, but only the Son." *[quote exact]*

### Was the voice from heaven saying 'Thou art my Son' the voice of the whole Trinity or of the Father only?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.; raised by the author): The Father only. ## Did Jesus claim to be God?

*Covers Jesus' own testimony to his divinity and pre-existence, including John 10:30, 'I and my Father are one', and the Jews' response to it. It also asks why his divinity was not openly revealed before his passion.*

**How the authors compare:** Owen and Athanasius agree that Jesus claimed deity in saying "I and the Father are one," and that his Jewish opponents' charge of making himself God shows what he claimed. They use that hostile reaction differently. Owen treats the Jews as having rightly understood Christ's meaning; on that basis he argues from John 10:30 and "Before Abraham was, I am" that Christ openly testified to his deity and pre-existence, and he sets this within a mediatorial account of why that glory was otherwise veiled until the resurrection. Athanasius instead stresses that the Pharisees' claimed zeal for God's honour was pretended and that they were the real blasphemers. He links their error to Arius, who uses Scripture language yet counts the Son among the creatures, while Owen links it to Cerinthus and the Ebionites. Owen alone takes up the questions of concealment and Christ's own open testimony.

### Does Jesus' statement "I and my Father are one" (John 10:30) assert that he is God?

*exegetical*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (The Preface; raised by the author): Yes. The Jews who heard it understood it well enough as a plain assertion of his being God, which is why they took up stones and charged him with blasphemy because, being a man, he made himself God. The Jews still hold to this day that he declared himself to be God and was slain for it. Scripture: John 10:30-33.
  > "They understood well enough the meaning of those words, “I and my Father are one,” — namely, that they were a plain assertion of his being God." *[quote exact]*

### Why was the knowledge of Christ's divine nature largely concealed from most people before his passion and resurrection?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (The Preface; raised by the author): It suited God's dispensation for the work Christ had to fulfil as mediator. Though in the form of God and equal with God, he emptied himself by taking the form of a servant so that he might be obedient unto death; his divine glory was veiled for a season until he was declared the Son of God with power by the resurrection and glorified with the glory he had with the Father before the world was. Scripture: Philippians 2:6-8, Romans 1:4, John 17:5, Isaiah 8:13-14.
  > "It was, indeed, suited, in the dispensation of God, unto the work that the Lord Jesus had to fulfil in the world, that, before his passion and resurrection, the knowledge of his divine nature, as unto his individual person, should be concealed from the most of men." *[quote exact]*

### Did Jesus himself openly testify to his divine nature and his pre-existence before the incarnation?

*exegetical*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (The Preface; raised by the author): Yes. Although his glory was veiled, as occasion required he gave plain and open testimony to his divine nature and eternal pre-existence, as when he said, "Before Abraham was, I am." It was precisely these assertions of his Deity that most enraged the Jews, who sought to stone him. Scripture: John 8:56-59.
  > "he forbare not to give plain and open testimony to his own divine nature and eternal pre-existence unto his incarnation." *[quote exact]*

*exegetical*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (The Preface; raised by the author): Yes. Owen says the Jews would have slain the Saviour precisely for bearing witness to his own Deity, and that they had a right apprehension of his mind in that testimony. Cerinthus and the Ebionites therefore persisted in the Jews' heresy despite the evidence of Christ's own witness.
  > "the Jews, who would have slain our Saviour for bearing witness to his own Deity, notwithstanding the evidence of that testimony, and the right apprehension which the Jews had of his mind therein" *[quote exact]*

*exegetical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Introduction. ## What is humanity's plight in sin, and how did Christ remedy it?

*Covers the consequences of sin and humanity's inability to cure its own corruption. It also asks whether God's justice required satisfaction and whether Christ made it, against Socinus.*

**How the authors compare:** Edward Henry Bickersteth and John Owen take up different halves of this topic and never answer the same question, so they neither directly agree nor directly conflict. Bickersteth describes the plight: sin brings moral, spiritual and bodily death, and for the impenitent the second death. He holds that it cannot be cured by human power, and he rests these points almost entirely on Scripture texts such as 'dying thou shalt die,' 'Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one,' and the Ethiopian who cannot change his skin. Owen addresses the remedy. He argues that Christ saves by fully satisfying God's justice, with that satisfaction imputed to believers through faith, and that God's essential holiness and justice as governor make punishment of sin indispensable, so that pardon without satisfaction is impossible. Owen's emphasis is polemical and doctrinal: he appeals to Scripture but also to the attributes of God's nature and to the earlier anti-Socinian defenders, and he rejects Socinus, Crellius and those schoolmen who allowed pardon without satisfaction, whereas Bickersteth names no opponents.

### What are the consequences of sin for humanity before God?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 1. Introduction. Preparation of heart. Our position before God.; raised by the author): Sin is fatal. The warning to Adam, 'dying thou shalt die,' was fulfilled at the fall: moral and spiritual death spread through the human soul, and bodily disease and decay are signs of that deeper malady. Death passes on all because all have sinned. For those who die in their sins, bodily death leads into the second death, the lake of fire. Scripture: Gen 2:17, James 1:15, Rom 5:12, Rev 20:14-15.
  > "And sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death. Death passes upon all men, for that all have sinned." *[quote exact]*

### Can human beings cure or remedy their own sinful corruption by their own power?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 1. Introduction. Preparation of heart. Our position before God.; raised by the author): No. Sin is not self-remedial; as far as man's own resources go, it is incurable. Scripture asks who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean and answers, 'Not one.' The Ethiopian can no more change his skin than those accustomed to evil can do good. Scripture: Job 14:4, Job 25:4, Jer 13:23.
  > "This evil is not self-remedial; but so far as lies in man, incurable. Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one." *[quote exact]*

### Is Jesus Christ our Saviour because he made satisfaction to God's justice for our sins, or only because he declared and exemplified the way of eternal salvation, as Socinus held?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader; raised by Socinians/Unitarians (Socinus, Crellius)): Christ is Saviour because he fully satisfied the justice of God for our sins, and this satisfaction is imputed to believers through faith. Owen rejects Socinus's view that Christ saves merely by declaring, confirming, and exemplifying the way of salvation and by raising us to life, with no satisfaction made or needed. He regards the orthodox statement defended by Covetus as the true state of the question, and notes that Crellius only disguises Socinus's view with orthodox-sounding words.
  > "Jesus Christ is therefore our Saviour, because he has satisfied the justice of God, by which we, being sinners, deserved to be condemned for all our sins" *[quote exact]*

### Do God's essential holiness and justice indispensably require that sin be punished, so that God could not simply pardon sin without any satisfaction?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader; raised by Socinians/Unitarians (Socinus; also schoolmen such as Aquinas, Durandus, Biel, Tataretus)): Yes. Owen holds that God's essential holiness and his justice as supreme governor of all indispensably require that sin should not go absolutely unpunished, and that leaving it unpunished would be repugnant to those holy properties of his nature. He says this principle is fully confirmed in Scripture, was the foundation of the first defenders against Socinus, and that abandoning it, as some schoolmen did by granting a possibility of pardon without satisfaction, opens the way to the Socinian error and leaves the truth weakened or even indefensible.
  > "the essential holiness of God with his justice or righteousness, as the supreme governor of all, did indispensably require that sin should not absolutely go unpunished; and that it should do so, stands in a repugnancy to those holy properties of his nature" *[quote exact]*

## Why does Scripture speak of God and divine names in the singular for the three persons?

*Covers why 'God' and names like Good, Holy and Judge are used in the singular of Father, Son and Spirit. It also covers why the Father is often called simply 'God'.*

**How the authors compare:** Gregory of Nyssa, who supplies most of the answers here, holds consistently that Scripture says 'God' only in the singular, though it freely says 'men' in the plural, because the plural 'men' misleads no one about there being many humanities, while the plural 'Gods' would suggest different natures in the divine essence. He grounds this in a principle about scriptural usage: Scripture follows common speech where no harm follows but speaks exactly where truth is at risk. He cites 'the Lord our God is one Lord,' and extends the same singular usage to all divine names such as 'Good,' 'Holy,' 'Saviour,' and 'Judge.' He declines to dispute whether these names refer to nature or to operation. Jonathan Edwards takes up a different question, why the Father in particular is so often called simply 'God,' and grounds his answer not in the oneness of nature but in the Father's place as fountain of the Godhead and in his economical office of upholding the rights of the Deity. Edwards, like Gregory, insists that this does not make the Father alone truly God, but his emphasis falls on order and office within the Trinity, whereas Gregory's falls on guarding the unity of the divine nature in language.

### Why does Scripture freely speak of 'men' in the plural but use the word 'God' only in the singular for Father, Son, and Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector): Scripture often follows common usage where no harm follows, as in saying 'men' or 'sons of men,' because no one is led to imagine many humanities. Where truth is at risk it speaks exactly. It therefore uses 'God' only in the singular, so as not to suggest different natures in the divine essence, since in the Godhead there is no difference of nature or operation. Scripture: Hebrews 6:16, Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "But the word “God” it employs studiously in the singular form only, guarding against introducing the idea of different natures in the Divine essence by the plural signification of “Gods.”" *[quote exact-context]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Because plural 'men' misleads no one into imagining many human natures, whereas plural 'Gods' would risk introducing the idea of different natures in the Divine essence. Where there is risk of injury to the truth, Scripture does not use words indiscriminately. Hence it says 'the Lord our God is one Lord.' Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "But the word “God” it employs studiously in the singular form only, guarding against introducing the idea of different natures in the Divine essence by the plural signification of “Gods.”" *[quote exact]*

### Why is the Father so often called simply 'God' in Scripture, and does this mean only He is properly God?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): The Father is called God without addition because He is the fountain of the Godhead and in the economy sustains the dignity of the Deity, upholding the rights of the Godhead; He is God not only by essence but by His economical office. This does not mean He alone is truly God, which is the mistaken inference some have drawn.
  > "Hereby we see how the Father is the fountain of the Godhead, and why when He is spoken of in Scripture He is so often, without any addition or distinction, called God, which has led some to think that He only was truly and properly God." *[quote exact]*

### Are divine names like 'Good,' 'Holy,' 'Saviour,' and 'Judge' to be used in the singular of the Trinity?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Yes. As the divine nature is called one nature, so it is called in the singular by all its other names. This holds whether the names refer to nature or to operation, a point Gregory declines to dispute.
  > "as it is called one nature, so it is called in the singular by all its other names, “God,” “Good,” “Holy,” “Saviour,” “Just,” “Judge,” and every other Divine name conceivable" *[quote exact]*

## Is the Son a creature or truly begotten of the Father's essence?

*Covers whether Christ is a created work or a genuine Son proper to the Father's essence, and whether all being divides into God and creature with nothing in between.*

**How the authors compare:** Athanasius and Augustine agree that the Son is not a creature but is of the Father's own substance, but they reach this by different routes. Athanasius argues chiefly from Scripture: the Father's own words 'This is My beloved Son' must be believed as spoken, and Scripture presents the Son as natural, genuine, only-begotten Wisdom and Word, proper to the Father's essence. On those grounds he rejects the Arian claim, as in the Thalia, that the Son came to be out of nothing like other things. Augustine instead reasons from a metaphysical division with no middle term: every substance is either God or a creature. Since the Son is uncreated, he must be of the same substance as the Father. Athanasius stresses faith in the divine word and the Arians' lack of any scriptural pretext, while Augustine stresses the logical impossibility of any intermediate kind of being.

*exegetical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > The Importance of the Subject. Restatement of Arianism in contrast, that He is a creature with a beginning: the controversy comes to this issue, whether one whom we are to believe in as God, can be so in name only, and is merely a creature. What pretence then for being indifferent in the controversy? The Arians rely on state patronage, and dare not avow their tenets.; raised by Arians): Yes. Anyone who has put faith in God must believe what God says. When the Father calls Him 'My beloved Son,' it is faithless to reinterpret this to mean that He is not a Son but a creature. Scripture gives the Arians no pretext for their doctrine, which is alien to the divine oracles. Scripture: Matthew 3:17.
  > "or who is there, who to the God in whom he has put faith, refuses to give credit, when He says, ‘This is My beloved Son[^1879],’ on the pretence that He is not a Son, but a creature?" *[quote exact]*

### Is the Son of God a creature or work, or an offspring proper to the Father's essence?

*theological*

## What do the Holy Spirit's visible signs and images reveal?

*Covers the dove at Jesus' baptism and the tongues of fire at Pentecost, and whether images of colored light such as the rainbow symbolize the Spirit and his graces.*

**How the authors compare:** Augustine and Jonathan Edwards address different questions here, so they do not directly agree or disagree. Augustine is concerned with which person appeared: he holds that the dove at the baptism and the tongues of fire at Pentecost were the Holy Spirit alone, not the whole Trinity. Edwards is concerned with what the signs mean. In both of his answers he reads the dove descending from the Father onto the Son as the visible emblem of the Father's infinite love for the Son, matching the voice's declaration "This is My well Beloved Son." He grounds this in Scripture's use of the dove as an emblem of love, especially in the Song of Solomon. Edwards also extends the theme to colored light, such as the rainbow, the light around God's throne and Christ's head, and the dove's feathers in Psalm 68, which he takes to represent the Spirit's graces because creation was designed to typify spiritual things. Augustine's answer, as given, offers no such symbolic or typological reading.

## Do the Son's worship and omnipresence prove he is God?

*Considers whether worship of the Son, given the rejection of creature-worship, and his omnipresence show that he is truly divine.*

**How the authors compare:** Daniel Waterland and Athanasius agree that the worship given to the Son shows he is truly God, but they make the case differently and answer slightly different questions. He argues from the internal consistency of Christian teaching: Christians were called away from worshipping creation, so worshipping the Son as a creature would contradict that rejection, and calling him a creature is therefore foreign to the faith. Waterland thus builds a positive proof from worship and divine attributes, while Athanasius uses worship to refute the claim that the Son is a creature.

## Does John's prologue teach the eternal deity of the Word?

*Covers how John 1:1-14 teaches that the Word is distinct from God and yet God. It also covers whether this settles the controversy for those who accept Scripture.*

## How is the Son the image and face of the Father?

*Covers John 14:9, 'He that hath seen Me hath seen the Father', and Christ's titles as the face or presence of God. It asks what these reveal about the Son sharing the Father's nature.*

### Why is Christ called the 'face' or 'presence' of God, and the angel of His presence?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Because the Son is God's own perfect idea of Himself, in which God continually views His own essence. This idea is the face God sees, as a man sees his own face in a looking glass. It is what God beholds most eminently, and so it is eminently in His presence. Scripture: Exodus 33:14, Isaiah 63:9.
  > "this idea is that "face of God" which God sees as a man sees his own face in a looking glass. 'Tis of such form or appearance whereby God eternally appears to Himself." *[quote exact]*

## Is the Holy Spirit the love and delight of God?

*Covers the scriptural and Augustinian case that the Spirit proceeds as the mutual love, bond, joy and delight of Father and Son, rather than as God's understanding. Texts discussed include 1 John 4 and Psalm 36's river of pleasures.*

### Does Scripture treat the love found in Christians as the same thing as the Holy Spirit dwelling in them?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. Scripture repeatedly speaks of Christian love as if it were the Spirit in believers, or at least the Spirit's first and most natural breathing in the soul. Examples include the "love of the Spirit" (Romans 15:30), "love in the Spirit" (Colossians 1:8), and love shed abroad in the heart by the Holy Ghost (Romans 5:5). In Galatians 5, "walk in the Spirit" restates the preceding exhortation to serve one another by love. Scripture: Philippians 2:1, 2 Corinthians 6:6, Romans 15:30, Colossians 1:8, Romans 5:5, Galatians 5:13-16.
  > "The Scripture seems in many places to speak of love in Christians as if it were the same with the Spirit of God in them, or at least as the prime and most natural breathing and acting of the Spirit in the soul." *[quote exact]*

### Does Scripture treat "good things" and "the Holy Spirit" as equivalent expressions?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. Matthew 7:11 promises that the Father will give the Holy Spirit to those who ask, where elsewhere "good things" are promised. The spring and rivers of living water in John 4 and 7 are expressly said to be the Spirit. The river of the water of life in Revelation 22 is likewise the Holy Ghost, the sum of all happiness in the world to come. Scripture: Matthew 7:11, John 4:10, John 7:38, John 7:39, Revelation 22:1, Revelation 22:17.
  > "The Holy Ghost is the sum of all good things. Good things and the Holy Spirit are synonymous expressions in Scripture: (Matt. 7:11) "How much more shall your Heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask Him."" *[quote exact]*

### Do the scriptural metaphors for the Holy Spirit (water, fire, breath, wind, oil, being poured out and breathed forth) show that the Spirit is God's love rather than his understanding?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. Such metaphors fit naturally only with affection: love or joy may be said to flow out like water, be breathed forth, be warm like fire, or be shed abroad. It would not sound natural to say this of an idea, judgment, perception or reason. So these images agree with the Spirit being God's love and delight, not his idea or knowledge. Scripture: Romans 5:5.
  > "The affection, love or joy, may be said to flow out as water or to be breathed forth as breath or wind. But it would [not] sound so well to say that an idea or judgment flows out or is breathed forth." *[quote exact]*

### Why does Scripture never speak of the Holy Spirit loving or being loved by the Father and the Son, though it often speaks of the love of the Father and of the Son?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Because the Holy Spirit is the divine love itself, the love of the Father and the Son. That is why Christ promises that he and the Father will love believers (John 14:21, 23) and makes no mention of the Spirit's love: the Spirit is that love rather than another lover alongside them. Scripture: John 14:21, John 14:23.
  > "This I suppose to be the reason why we have never any account of the Holy Ghost's loving either the Father or the Son, or of the Son's or the Father's loving the Holy Ghost" *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): No. Scripture never says God loves the Holy Ghost and never gives Him epithets of love. It speaks of no acceptance, reward or mutual friendship involving Him, and gives no command to love Him. The Son, by contrast, is called God's Elect, the Beloved, the one in whom He is well pleased, and the Only Begotten. These titles present the Son as the object of the Father's love as if no other person shared it. Edwards treats this as evidence about the Spirit's distinct place in the Godhead. Scripture: Matthew 3:17, Isaiah 42:1, John 3:16.
  > "God is never said to love the Holy Ghost nor are any epithets that betoken love anywhere given to Him, tho so many are ascribed to the Son, as God's Elect, The Beloved, He in Whom God's soul delights, He in Whom He is well pleased" *[quote exact]*

### Does 1 John 4:8-13 teach that the Holy Spirit is the love of God?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. Verse 12 argues that if love dwells in us, God dwells in us. Verse 13 shows the force of that argument by grounding it in God's having given us His Spirit, so the love that dwells in us is God's Spirit. The passage also takes for granted that God's Spirit is God, and 1 John 3:24 makes the same point in the same way. Scripture: 1 John 4:8, 1 John 4:12, 1 John 4:13, 1 John 3:24.
  > "In the 12th verse the apostle argues that if we have love dwelling in us we have God dwelling in us, and in the 13th verse He clears the force of the argument by this that love is God's Spirit." *[quote exact]*

### Do the Holy Spirit's title "Comforter" and the biblical phrase "joy in the Holy Ghost" support the idea that the Spirit is God's joy and delight?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. The Spirit's office of comforting and delighting God's people fits well with the Spirit being God's own joy and delight. Texts such as "joy of the Holy Ghost" and "filled with joy and with the Holy Ghost" mean that believers are filled with spiritual joy, which is the Spirit. Scripture: 1 Thessalonians 1:6, Romans 14:17, Acts 9:31, Acts 13:52.
  > "But how well doth this agree with the Holy Ghost being God's joy and delight, (Acts 13:52) "And the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Ghost"" *[quote exact]*

### Does Psalm 36:7-9 show that God's loving-kindness is the same as the Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. The loving-kindness, the fatness of God's house, the river of God's pleasures, the fountain of life and God's light in this psalm are all the same thing. Christ himself teaches that spiritual fountains and rivers of living water mean the Holy Spirit (John 4:14; 7:38-39). Therefore the Holy Spirit is God's loving-kindness and delight. This river is also the same as the river of the water of life proceeding from the throne in Revelation 22. Scripture: Psalm 36:7-9, John 4:14, John 7:38-39, Revelation 22:1, Revelation 21:23-24, Revelation 22:5.
  > "But Christ Himself expressly teaches us that by spiritual fountains and rivers of water of life is meant the Holy Ghost." *[quote exact]*

### Why should the Holy Spirit be identified with the divine will or act, rather than with God's understanding?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Because the only act in God is the act of the will. The Son is God's idea of Himself, which belongs to the understanding. The Spirit is the Deity in act, and since there is no act other than the act of the will, the Spirit is the divine will, that is, love.
  > "there proceeds the third Person in the Trinity, the Holy Spirit, viz., the Deity in act, for there is no other act but the act of the will." *[quote exact]*

### If the Holy Spirit is the divine love or will, how can the Spirit be a person with understanding?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): The three Persons mutually indwell one another, so each has what the others are. The Spirit understands because the Son, the divine Idea, is in him. The Father understands because the Son is in him and loves because the Spirit is in him. The divine love is no blind love: it is the love of the understanding both objectively and subjectively, so the Spirit truly knows and searches the deep things of God. Scripture: John 14:10, 1 Corinthians 2:10.
  > "the Holy Ghost or the Divine essence subsisting is Divine, but understands because the Son the Divine Idea is in Him" *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. The Spirit is the love of the understanding both objectively and subjectively, so it is not a blind love; the Son, the Divine understanding, is in the Spirit as flowing out in Him, though the Spirit does not proceed from Him. Hence Scripture rightly says the Spirit knows and searches all things, even the deep things of God. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 2:10.
  > "The understanding is so in the Spirit that the Spirit may be said to know, as the Spirit of God is truly and perfectly said to know and to search all things, even the deep things of God." *[quote exact]*

### Is it unreasonable to say that a divine Person has understanding or love by another Person's being in Him?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by unnamed objector): No. Scripture gives ground for it, since it teaches that the Son is the wisdom, reason and truth of God, so the Father is wise by His own wisdom, the Son, being in Him. Likewise understanding is in the Spirit because the Son is in Him. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 1:24, John 1:1, John 14:6.
  > "we have Scripture ground to conclude so concerning the Father's having wisdom and understanding or reason that it is by the Son's being in Him; because we are there informed that He is the wisdom and reason and truth of God" *[quote exact]*

### How does the Holy Spirit proceed within the Godhead according to the psychological model of the Trinity?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): The Holy Spirit proceeds as the mutual love and delight between the Father and the Son. Once the Son is begotten as God's perfect idea of Himself, an infinitely holy energy arises between Father and Son in loving and delighting in each other. In this act the divine essence flows out and is breathed forth in love, so that the Godhead stands forth in a third manner of subsistence, the Spirit. Scripture: Proverbs 8:30.
  > "there proceeds a most pure act, and an infinitely holy and sacred energy arises between the Father and Son in mutually loving and delighting in each other, for their love and joy is mutual" *[quote exact]*

### Does the saints' communion with God confirm that the Holy Spirit is God's love and delight?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. Communion is a common partaking of good, whether excellency or happiness. Saints partake of the divine nature, holiness, glory and joy of the Father and Son, and this communion consists in partaking of the Holy Spirit. Therefore the Spirit is God's own beauty and happiness, that is, his love and joy. Scripture: 1 John 1:3, 2 Peter 1:4, Hebrews 12:10, John 17:22-23, John 17:13.
  > "It is a confirmation that the Holy Ghost is God's love and delight, because the saints communion with God consists in their partaking of the Holy Ghost." *[quote exact]*

### Why is the river in Psalm 36:8 called "the river of God's pleasures," and what is it?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): It is the Holy Spirit. The Spirit is the infinite delight and pleasure of God, and so the river is called the river of God's pleasures rather than God's river of pleasures. It is the same as the river of the water of life proceeding from the throne of the Father and the Son in Revelation 22. John himself interprets rivers of living water as the Spirit. Scripture: Psalm 36:8, Revelation 22:1, John 7:38-39.
  > "the Holy Ghost being the infinite delight and pleasure of God, the river is called the river of God's pleasures (Ps. 36:8), not God's river of pleasures" *[quote exact]*

### What is the bond that unites the Father and the Son?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): The Holy Spirit. He is their common excellency, joy and happiness in which they are all united, the bond of perfectness by which they are one. Our communion with the Father and the Son likewise consists in drinking into the same Spirit.
  > "This is the common excellency and joy and happiness in which they all are united; 'tis the bond of perfectness by which they are one in the Father and the Son" *[quote exact]*

## Is the Son eternal, or was there a time when he was not?

*Covers scriptural arguments for the Son's eternity and co-existence with the Father, and the rebuttal of Arian slogans that the Son once was not or came from nothing. It also asks whether God was always Father.*

### Does Scripture teach that the Son is eternal and always coexistent with the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by the author): Yes. No Scripture says 'once he was not' of the Saviour; it speaks of him as 'always' and 'eternal.' John says 'In the beginning was the Word.' Revelation calls him the one 'who is and who was and who is to come,' and no one can take eternity away from 'who is' and 'who was.' Paul calls Christ 'God blessed for ever.' Scripture: John 1:1, Revelation 1:8, Romans 9:5.
  > "no holy Scripture has used such language of the Saviour, but rather ‘always’ and ‘eternal’ and ‘coexistent always with the Father.’" *[quote exact]*

### If the Son is eternal and coexists with the Father, does that make him the Father's brother rather than his Son?

*logical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): No. The objection would have weight only if the Son were said merely to be eternally with the Father. But he is confessed both as eternal and as Son begotten from the Father, and one who is begotten cannot be the brother of the one who begets him. Nor can the Word be the brother of the one whose Word he is.
  > "if, while we say that He is eternal, we also confess Him to be Son from the Father, how can He that is begotten be considered brother of Him who begets?" *[quote exact-context]*

### Does the Son's being called the radiance of the Father's glory prove that the Son is eternal?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): Yes. Light is never seen without the brightness of its radiance. So, since the Father is the Everlasting God and the Son is His radiance, no one can say of the Son 'There was once when He was not' or 'Before His generation He was not.' Scripture: Hebrews 1:3, Psalm 90:17, Psalm 36:9, Isaiah 40:28.
  > "for when did man see light without the brightness of its radiance, that he may say of the Son, ‘There was once, when He was not,’ or ‘Before His generation He was not.’" *[quote exact]*

### Do Christ's sayings in the form 'I am' (the Truth, the Shepherd, the Light) show that the Son is eternal and without beginning?

*exegetical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by the author): Yes. The Lord says 'I am the Truth,' not 'I became the Truth,' and He always speaks in the form 'I am.' Coming from God, the Wisdom and Word of the Father, this language signifies that the Son is eternal and without beginning. Scripture: John 14:6, John 10:11, John 8:12, John 13:13.
  > "the Lord Himself says, ‘I am the Truth,’ not ‘I became the Truth;’ but always, ‘I am,—I am the Shepherd,—I am the Light,’" *[quote exact]*

### Is the Son eternal, never having not been, or did he come into existence out of nothing?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by the author): The Son is eternal and did not come out of nothing. Scripture uses temporal terms of creatures but 'ever' of the Word. Therefore the Son is the Father's eternal Image and Word, always existing, as the eternal Radiance of an eternal Light. Scripture: Hebrews 1:3.
  > "the Son did not come out of nothing, nor is in the number of originated things at all, but is the Father’s Image and Word eternal, never having not been, but being ever, as the eternal Radiance of a Light which is eternal." *[quote exact]*

### Does denying that the Word of God eternally exists, and imagining instead a Son who once was not, amount to turning away from the true Word to something that is nothing?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > Extracts from the Thalia of Arius. Arius maintains that God became a Father, and the Son was not always; the Son out of nothing; once He was not; He was not before his generation; He was created; named Wisdom and Word after God's attributes; made that He might make us; one out of many powers of God; alterable; exalted on God's foreknowledge of what He was to be; not very God; but called so as others by participation; foreign in essence from the Father; does not know or see the Father; does not know Himself.; raised by the author): Yes. The Word of God truly is, so whoever turns away from him and fashions for himself a Word who once was not has abandoned the real Word for a fiction, and so falls into what is nothing. Athanasius applies Hosea's words, 'they turn away to nothing', to the Arians, who have been redeemed by the Lord yet speak lies against him. Scripture: Hosea 7:13, Hosea 7:15, Hosea 7:16.
  > "For to turn away from the Word of God, which is, and to fashion to themselves one that is not, is to fall to what is nothing." *[quote exact]*

### Do the Arian formulas 'there was once when the Son was not' and 'He was not before His generation' imply a time before the Son?

*logical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): No. If 'once' refers to the Father, it is impious, since the Father is ever and cannot be signified by 'once'; if it refers to the Son, it is absurd, since the Son cannot both be and not be. The only remaining sense is that there was a time when the Word was not, which implies a time before the Word.
  > "But if ye say that the Son was once, when He Himself was not, the answer is foolish and unmeaning. For how could He both be and not be?" *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): No. Both formulas mean the same thing. Each signifies that there is a time before the Word, which Athanasius rejects as unscriptural.
  > "And your other, ‘The Son was not before His generation,’ is equivalent to saying, ‘There was once when He was not,’ for both the one and the other signify that there is a time before the Word." *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. When the Arians say 'There was when He was not' and 'He was not before His generation,' they still mean times, so the formula implies a temporal interval before the Son.
  > "For you still simply mean times, when you say, ‘There was when He was not,’ and ‘He was not before His generation.’" *[quote exact]*

### Does Scripture apply phrases like 'once was not', 'before', and 'when' to the Son of God, or only to creatures?

*exegetical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Of the Word, Scripture says 'ever' and 'I am'. So these terms are alien to the Word, and the Son is not among originated things. Scripture: Genesis 2:5, Deuteronomy 32:8, Proverbs 8:23-25, Jeremiah 1:5, Psalm 90:2, John 8:58.
  > "Thus it appears that the phrases ‘once was not,’ and ‘before it came to be,’ and ‘when,’ and the like, belong to things originate and creatures, which come out of nothing, but are alien to the Word." *[quote exact]*

### Was there a time when the Son was not, and did God become Father only afterwards?

*theological*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > The Importance of the Subject. ### Can there have been a time or age before the Son existed, given that the ages were made through him?

*philosophical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by the author): No. The ages and all things were made through the Word, and without him nothing was made. ### Was there any interval or time before the Word existed?

*philosophical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): No. Every interval in the ages is measured, and the Word is King and Maker of all the ages. ## Can saving trust be placed in anyone but God?

*Covers the scriptural link between God as sole Creator and sole Saviour, God's jealousy for supreme trust and worship, and what trusting Christ for salvation implies about his deity.*

### Why does Scripture ground God's sole power to save in his being the Creator?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 2. That Scripture, in the Old and the New Testament alike, detaches our ultimate confidence from man the creature, and attaches it to God the Creator; – by contrasting the sinfulness and feebleness of mortal man with the goodness and omnipotence of the Eternal Jehovah: by direct prohibition and precept: by exhibiting the holy Jealousy of God.; raised by the author): Because creative power is presented as proof of God's infinite preeminence: only the Maker is mighty to save. Isaiah rebukes those who fear dying man and forget the Lord their Maker who stretched out the heavens. If this holds for temporal deliverances, it holds still more for eternal salvation, since no man can redeem his brother but God alone redeems the soul from the grave. Scripture: Isaiah 51:12-13, Psalm 49:7-8, Psalm 49:15, Isaiah 31:3.
  > "Observe, in all these passages, how much stress is laid on the creative power of God as proof of his infinite preeminence. The Maker alone is mighty to save." *[quote exact]*

### Does Scripture teach that ultimate trust and confidence must be placed in God the Creator alone rather than in any creature?

*exegetical*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 2. That Scripture, in the Old and the New Testament alike, detaches our ultimate confidence from man the creature, and attaches it to God the Creator; – by contrasting the sinfulness and feebleness of mortal man with the goodness and omnipotence of the Eternal Jehovah: by direct prohibition and precept: by exhibiting the holy Jealousy of God.; raised by the author): Yes. Scripture consistently withdraws ultimate confidence from man the creature and places it in God the Creator. ## How does a shared nature in many individuals bear on the one divine nature in three persons?

*Covers the analogy of three men sharing one nature, whether a common nature is divided or multiplied in individuals, and whether a singular collective noun can name several who share one nature.*

### If Peter, James and John, who share one human nature, are called three men, why are Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who share one divine nature, not called three Gods?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector (Ablabius, reporting opponents' argument)): Calling those who share one nature 'many men' is itself a customary abuse of language, so it gives no ground for saying 'three Gods.' The name 'man' properly signifies the common nature, not the individual, which is why we address a particular person by his proper name rather than by the common name. Strictly speaking, saying 'many men' is like saying 'many human natures,' though the nature is one. The analogy therefore does not oblige us to multiply the divine name with the Persons.
  > "is a customary abuse of language, and that it would be much the same thing to say they are “many human natures.”" *[quote exact-context]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector (Ablabius' opponents)): No. Saying "many men" is already a misuse of language. It is tolerated only because it is a harmless habit that is hard to change. In speech about the Divine nature such loose usage is dangerous, so the error should not be carried over. We must confess one God, even though the name of Godhead extends through the Trinity. Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "in the case of the statement concerning the Divine nature the various use of terms is no longer so free from danger: for that which is of small account is in these subjects no longer a small matter" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector (adversaries who hold that Godhead signifies nature)): No. A nature is neither diminished nor increased by being contemplated in more or fewer individuals. Only things individually circumscribed by body, size, place, figure and colour are counted by addition. The divine nature is free from such circumscription, so it is not enumerated and cannot be contemplated as a multitude.
  > "That which is not thus circumscribed is not enumerated, and that which is not enumerated cannot be contemplated in multitude." *[quote exact]*

### Is it strictly correct to call Peter, James and John "many men", given that they share one human nature?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. Calling those who are undivided in nature by the name of their common nature in the plural is a customary abuse of language, equivalent to saying "many human natures." The term "man" belongs to what is common, not to the individual as such, so strictly "man" is one even when many individuals share the nature.
  > "the practice of calling those who are not divided in nature by the very name of their common nature in the plural, and saying they are “many men,” is a customary abuse of language" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. Strictly speaking, the man in Peter, James, and John is one, though the individuals are many. Gold remains one gold even when struck into many staters: the coins are counted, not the material. Calling a shared nature by a plural name is a misuse sanctioned only by custom.
  > "As, then, the golden staters are many, but the gold is one, so too those who are exhibited to us severally in the nature of man, as Peter, James, and John, are many, yet the man in them is one." *[quote exact]*

### If the name 'Godhead' signifies an operation rather than a nature, should Father, Son and Holy Spirit be called three Gods, as several people sharing one occupation are called 'three orators'?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector): No. Among men, those who share a pursuit are rightly counted as many because each works separately by himself, with no part in the others' individual acts; one rhetorician pleads his own case and another pleads his. In the divine nature, however, the Father does nothing by himself in which the Son does not work jointly, nor does the Son act apart from the Spirit. Because the divine operation is one and undivided across the three Persons, the Deity is rightly spoken of in the singular as one God and one Godhead.
  > "But in the case of the Divine nature we do not similarly learn that the Father does anything by Himself in which the Son does not work conjointly" *[quote exact-context]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by unnamed objector): Because men sharing a pursuit each work separately, whereas the divine Persons never act separately. Each rhetorician pleads on his own account, so they are properly counted as many. But the Father does nothing in which the Son does not work conjointly, nor the Son anything apart from the Spirit. Therefore a name derived from operation cannot be divided by the number of the Persons, since the result of their joint operation is one.
  > "so neither can we call those who exercise this Divine and superintending power and operation towards ourselves and all creation, conjointly and inseparably, by their mutual action, three Gods." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. Every good name, Godhead included, is brought to perfection from the Father through the Son in the Spirit without interval of time, and the unity of the action prevents plural enumeration. The name derived from an operation cannot be divided among many when the operation is one.
  > "if Godhead also is one of the good names and concepts, it would not be proper to divide the name into a plurality, since the unity existing in the action prevents plural enumeration" *[quote exact]*

### Is a common nature such as humanity multiplied or divided when it exists in many individuals?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): No. The nature is one, united in itself, an indivisible unit not increased by addition or diminished by subtraction. It remains one and continuous even though it appears in plurality, and it is not divided among the individuals who share in it. Only the persons, separated by their peculiar attributes, are counted by number.
  > "yet their nature is one, at union in itself, and an absolutely indivisible unit, not capable of increase by addition or of diminution by subtraction" *[quote exact]*

### Can a collective noun in the singular properly name a plurality of individuals sharing one nature?

*logical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'; raised by the author): Yes. We say "a people," "a mob," "an army" or "an assembly" in the singular, even though each is a plurality. In the same way, by the more accurate expression, "man" is one even when many individuals share the nature. This supports calling the Godhead one, though it is shared by three Persons.
  > "as we speak of a people, or a mob, or an army, or an assembly in the singular in every case, while each of these is conceived as being in plurality, so according to the more accurate expression, “man” would be said to be one" *[quote exact]*

## What analogies illustrate the Trinity?

## Why did God create the world, and how does creation relate to the Son?

### Was the world made especially for the Son of God?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. That means it is made to provide a spouse for Christ. The elect creatures whom wisdom chooses are the end for which the rest of creation exists.
  > "But Christ is Divine wisdom so that the world is made to gratify Divine love as exercised by Christ or to gratify the love that is in Christ's heart, or to provide a spouse for Christ." *[quote exact]*

## What does 'His eternal power' in Romans 1:20 refer to?

*Examines whether the phrase designates the Father himself or the Son, and what this implies for the Son's divinity.*

### Does 'His eternal power' in Romans 1:20 refer to the Father himself or to the Son?

*exegetical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): It refers to the Son. Paul elsewhere calls Christ 'the Power of God and the Wisdom of God.' The text says 'His power,' not 'God himself is the power,' and what is 'His' is not 'He,' though it is proper to him rather than alien. The context also shows that the Framer's Power seen in creation is the Word, through whom all things were made. Scripture: Romans 1:20, 1 Corinthians 1:24, 2 Corinthians 3:16-17.
  > "This is not so; for he says not, ‘God Himself is the power,’ but ‘His is the power.’ Very plain is it to all that ‘His’ is not ‘He;’ yet not something alien but rather proper to Him." *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'; raised by Arians): Yes. After mentioning the creation, Paul naturally speaks of the Framer's Power seen in it, and that Power is the Word through whom all things were made. Since the creatures witness to their Framer, Paul accuses the Greeks of contemplating creation's order without reflecting on the framing Word within it, and so by 'His eternal Power' he signifies the Son. Scripture: Romans 1:20, 1 Corinthians 1:24.
  > "he naturally speaks of the Framer’s Power as seen in it, which Power, I say, is the Word of God, by whom all things have been made." *[quote exact]*

## Do the three Persons dwell in one another?

*Covers the mutual indwelling (perichoresis) between the Son and the Father, and whether it extends to the Holy Spirit.*

### Does the mutual indwelling Christ describes between himself and the Father ("I am in the Father and the Father in Me") extend to all three Persons of the Trinity?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity; raised by the author): Yes. As Christ said, "I am in the Father and the Father in Me," so each Person is in each of the others. They are united in an ineffable way, so that one has another, they have communion in one another, and they are as it were predicable of one another. Scripture: John 14:10.

## Why did Paul preach only 'Christ crucified' if all wisdom is hidden in Christ?

*Covers why Paul limited his preaching to Christ crucified, and how that simple message relates to the deeper wisdom about Christ's divinity.*

### Why did Paul preach to the Corinthians only 'Jesus Christ and him crucified' if all the treasures of wisdom are hid in Christ?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > This Work is Written Against Those Who Sophistically Assail the Faith of the Trinity, Through Misuse of Reason. ## Why does God, as perfect love, require perfect love from his creatures?

*Covers the reasoning that God's perfection in love entails that he demands complete love in return.*

### Why must God, being perfect love, require the perfect love of his creatures?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Chapter 1. Introduction. Preparation of heart. Our position before God.; raised by the author): Because God is himself perfect love, he must of necessity require perfect love from his creatures, both for his own glory and for their happiness. The great commandment to love God wholly and one's neighbour as oneself sums up his righteous government. No other law can be conceived binding together the perfect society of heaven; it is the utterance of the mind of the blessed God. Scripture: Matthew 22:37-39.
  > "You acknowledge that He is himself perfect love, and must of necessity require the perfect love of his creatures for the sake of his own glory and of their happiness." *[quote exact]*
