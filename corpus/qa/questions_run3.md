## 234. Do the Father, Son and Holy Spirit work separately or as one indivisible operation, and how does each take part in it?

*theological*

**Across sources:** Gregory of Nyssa and Augustine agree that the three Persons do not act separately but work indivisibly. Gregory grounds this in the order of a single operation that issues from the Father, proceeds through the Son and is perfected in the Spirit, so it is never three actions. Augustine grounds it in the Persons' indivisibility of being, while also allowing that certain manifestations, such as the Son's birth and the Father's voice, belong to one Person alone.

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Every operation from God to creation originates from the Father, proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit. It is one motion and disposition of good will, communicated from the Father through the Son to the Spirit. It is not three separate actions matching the number of the Persons.
  > "every operation which extends from God to the Creation, and is named according to our variable conceptions of it, has its origin from the Father, and proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. All providence, care and superintendence is one operation, not three, directed by the Trinity but not divided according to the number of Persons. It issues from the Father as from a spring, is brought into operation by the Son, and is perfected by the power of the Spirit, so that no act is the work of one Person in isolation. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 12:11.
  > "being, indeed, directed by the Holy Trinity, yet not severed by a threefold division according to the number of the Persons contemplated in the Faith, so that each of the acts, contemplated by itself, should be the work of the Father alone" *[quote exact]*
- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > What the Doctrine of the Catholic Faith is Concerning the Trinity.): Indivisibly. Just as the three Persons are indivisible in being, so they work indivisibly. Augustine affirms this together with the fact that certain manifestations belong to one Person alone, such as the Son's birth and the Father's voice.
  > "although the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, as they are indivisible, so work indivisibly" *[quote exact]*

## 292. Does Romans 11:36 ("of Him, and through Him, and to/in Him, are all things") refer to the Father alone, or to the Father, Son and Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

**Across sources:** Augustine and Edwards both read the verse in Trinitarian terms, assigning "of Him" to the Father, "through Him" to the Son and "in Him" to the Holy Spirit. Their emphases differ. Augustine argues from the singular "to whom be glory" and the opening reference to the wisdom "of God" that the three are one God. Edwards applies the pattern to redemption: God is the one of whom our good is purchased, the purchaser, and the good purchased, since the Spirit is himself that good.

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. Not Only the Father, But the Trinity, is Affirmed to Be Immortal. All Things are Not from the Father Alone, But Also from the Son. That the Holy Spirit is Very God, Equal with the Father and the Son.): It can be read of the three Persons as one God. If "of Him" is assigned to the Father, "through Him" to the Son, and "in Him" to the Holy Spirit, the singular "to whom be glory" shows that the three are one God. The passage also begins with the wisdom and knowledge "of God," not of any one Person. Scripture: Romans 11:33-36.
  > "it is manifest that the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit is one God, inasmuch as the words continue in the singular number, “To whom be glory for ever.”" *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. All our good is of God the Father, through God the Son, and in the Holy Ghost, since the Spirit is Himself all our good. So God is the one of whom the good is purchased, the purchaser, and the thing purchased, and is thus Alpha and Omega in redemption. Scripture: Romans 11:36, 1 Corinthians 8:6.
  > "All our good is of God the Father, it is all through God the Son, and all is in the Holy Ghost as He is Himself all our good." *[quote exact]*

## 231. If the name 'Godhead' signifies an operation rather than a nature, should Father, Son and Holy Spirit be called three Gods, as several people sharing one occupation are called 'three orators'?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Among men, those who share a pursuit are rightly counted as many because each works separately by himself, with no part in the others' individual acts; one rhetorician pleads his own case and another pleads his. In the divine nature, however, the Father does nothing by himself in which the Son does not work jointly, nor does the Son act apart from the Spirit. Because the divine operation is one and undivided across the three Persons, the Deity is rightly spoken of in the singular as one God and one Godhead.
  > "But in the case of the Divine nature we do not similarly learn that the Father does anything by Himself in which the Son does not work conjointly" *[quote exact-context]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Because men sharing a pursuit each work separately, whereas the divine Persons never act separately. Each rhetorician pleads on his own account, so they are properly counted as many. But the Father does nothing in which the Son does not work conjointly, nor the Son anything apart from the Spirit. Therefore a name derived from operation cannot be divided by the number of the Persons, since the result of their joint operation is one.
  > "so neither can we call those who exercise this Divine and superintending power and operation towards ourselves and all creation, conjointly and inseparably, by their mutual action, three Gods." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Every good name, Godhead included, is brought to perfection from the Father through the Son in the Spirit without interval of time, and the unity of the action prevents plural enumeration. The name derived from an operation cannot be divided among many when the operation is one.
  > "if Godhead also is one of the good names and concepts, it would not be proper to divide the name into a plurality, since the unity existing in the action prevents plural enumeration" *[quote exact]*

## 232. If Peter, James and John, who share one human nature, are called three men, why are Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who share one divine nature, not called three Gods?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Calling those who share one nature 'many men' is itself a customary abuse of language, so it gives no ground for saying 'three Gods.' The name 'man' properly signifies the common nature, not the individual, which is why we address a particular person by his proper name rather than by the common name. Strictly speaking, saying 'many men' is like saying 'many human natures,' though the nature is one. The analogy therefore does not oblige us to multiply the divine name with the Persons.
  > "is a customary abuse of language, and that it would be much the same thing to say they are “many human natures.”" *[quote exact-context]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Saying "many men" is already a misuse of language. It is tolerated only because it is a harmless habit that is hard to change. In speech about the Divine nature such loose usage is dangerous, so the error should not be carried over. We must confess one God, even though the name of Godhead extends through the Trinity. Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "in the case of the statement concerning the Divine nature the various use of terms is no longer so free from danger: for that which is of small account is in these subjects no longer a small matter" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. A nature is neither diminished nor increased by being contemplated in more or fewer individuals. Only things individually circumscribed by body, size, place, figure and colour are counted by addition. The divine nature is free from such circumscription, so it is not enumerated and cannot be contemplated as a multitude.
  > "That which is not thus circumscribed is not enumerated, and that which is not enumerated cannot be contemplated in multitude." *[quote exact]*

## 276. Do the Arian formulas 'there was once when the Son was not' and 'He was not before His generation' imply a time before the Son?

*logical*

- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. These attributes, being the points in dispute, are first proved by direct texts of Scripture. Concerning the 'eternal power' of God in Rom. i. 20, which is shewn to mean the Son. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'): No. If 'once' refers to the Father, it is impious, since the Father is ever and cannot be signified by 'once'; if it refers to the Son, it is absurd, since the Son cannot both be and not be. The only remaining sense is that there was a time when the Word was not, which implies a time before the Word.
  > "But if ye say that the Son was once, when He Himself was not, the answer is foolish and unmeaning. For how could He both be and not be?" *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. Remarks on the Arian formula, 'Once the Son was not,' its supporters not daring to speak of 'a time when the Son was not.'): No. Both formulas mean the same thing. Each signifies that there is a time before the Word, which Athanasius rejects as unscriptural.
  > "And your other, ‘The Son was not before His generation,’ is equivalent to saying, ‘There was once when He was not,’ for both the one and the other signify that there is a time before the Word." *[quote exact]*
- **Athanasius of Alexandria, *Four Discourses Against the Arians*** (Against the Arians. (Orationes contra Arianos IV.) > Discourse I > That the Son is Eternal and Increate. ## 233. Does the word "Godhead" (or any other divine name) signify or comprehend the very essence of the divine nature?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Following Scripture, the divine nature is unnameable and unspeakable; every term, whether invented by human custom or handed down in Scripture, explains our conceptions of the divine nature but does not include the signification of that nature itself. Each term applied to God has its own distinct sense that makes known something about the nature, not what it is in essence.
  > "every term either invented by the custom of men, or handed down to us by the Scriptures, is indeed explanatory of our conceptions of the Divine Nature, but does not include the signification of that nature itself" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. The divine nature is infinite and incomprehensible in every respect, and what is absolutely unlimited cannot be limited even by a name. The Deity is above every name, and since 'Godhead' is a name, it cannot express the nature itself. Gregory therefore takes it to signify an operation. Scripture: Philippians 2:9.
  > "we say that the Deity is above every name: and “Godhead” is a name. Now it cannot be that the same thing should at once be a name and be accounted as above every name." *[quote exact]*

## 235. If the Godhead belongs to Father, Son and Holy Spirit, must one say there are three Gods, or is there only one God?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Gregory treats both alternatives as erroneous: saying 'three Gods' is unlawful, and denying the Godhead of the Son and Spirit is impious and absurd. The faith confesses three Persons without difference of nature while holding that the Godhead is one, and he sets out to show this is not inconsistent.
  > "either to say “there are three Gods,” which is unlawful, or not to acknowledge the Godhead of the Son and the Holy Spirit, which is impious and absurd." *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. Scripture testifies, "Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one Lord." So we must confess one God, even though the name of Godhead extends through the Holy Trinity. This follows the same principle as in human nature: the name of the nature should not be put in the plural. Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4.
  > "Therefore we must confess one God, according to the testimony of Scripture, “Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one Lord,” even though the name of Godhead extends through the Holy Trinity." *[quote exact]*

## 243. Is the Holy Spirit's role in the work of redemption equal to that of the Father and the Son, or does he merely apply the benefits purchased by Christ?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. The Father appoints and provides the Redeemer, accepts the price and grants the thing purchased. The Son is the Redeemer and is himself the price. The Holy Ghost communicates the thing purchased by communicating Himself, and He is the thing purchased. Since the price and the thing bought with it are equal, the Spirit's share is not a lesser, merely subservient application of benefits.
  > "The Father appoints and provides the Redeemer, and Himself accepts the price and grants the thing purchased; the Son is the Redeemer by offering Himself and is the price; and the Holy Ghost immediately communicates to us the thing purchased by communicating Himself, and He is the thing purchased." *[quote exact]*
- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Not on the traditional view, but yes on Edwards's view. If the Spirit only hands to us the blessing after it is purchased, subservient to the other two, his part would be little compared to the Father giving his Son and the Son paying an infinite price. But if the Spirit is himself the love of God and the very thing purchased, there is an equality: the thing bought is equal to the price, and the glory of affording it is the glory of the thing's worth.
  > "But according to this there is an equality. To be the love of God to the world is as much as for the Father and the Son to do so much from love to the world, and to be the thing purchased was as much as to be the price." *[quote exact]*

## 245. Is it strictly correct to call Peter, James and John "many men", given that they share one human nature?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Calling those who are undivided in nature by the name of their common nature in the plural is a customary abuse of language, equivalent to saying "many human natures." The term "man" belongs to what is common, not to the individual as such, so strictly "man" is one even when many individuals share the nature.
  > "the practice of calling those who are not divided in nature by the very name of their common nature in the plural, and saying they are “many men,” is a customary abuse of language" *[quote exact]*
- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Strictly speaking, the man in Peter, James, and John is one, though the individuals are many. Gold remains one gold even when struck into many staters: the coins are counted, not the material. Calling a shared nature by a plural name is a misuse sanctioned only by custom.
  > "As, then, the golden staters are many, but the gold is one, so too those who are exhibited to us severally in the nature of man, as Peter, James, and John, are many, yet the man in them is one." *[quote exact]*

## 247. Does calling the Father 'the only true God' (John 17:3) exclude the Son from being true God?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. *exegetical*

## 266. Does Scripture's calling Christ 'the wisdom of God' show that the Son is God's eternal idea of Himself?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. God's wisdom or knowledge is the same as His perfect and eternal idea. ## 267. Does Novatian's remark on Philippians 2:6, that the Son 'never compared himself with God the Father', deny that the Son is equal to the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. Novatian gives the reason: the Son remembered that he was from his Father, that is, begotten and not unbegotten. The Son never claimed equality with the Father in respect of origin. He knew himself to be second in order, not the first Person of the Trinity. Hilary and Phoebadius use similar language and cannot be suspected of Arianism. Scripture: Philippians 2:6.
  > "He never pretended to an equality with the Father, in respect of his origina/, knowing himself to be second only in order, not the first Person of the ever blessed Trinity." *[quote exact]*

## 268. Does Novatian's statement that 'two found equal would have made two Gods' count against the full coequality of the Son with the Father?

*exegetical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. Read in full, it means only that if both were equal in origin, both unbegotten, there would be two Gods. Father and Son are not two Gods because they are not both unoriginated. This was the common Catholic answer to the charge of tritheism, both before and after Nicaea. Rightly understood, the passage is strong testimony for the coequality the orthodox defend; Whitby once read it as showing Christ equal to the Father in every respect except being God of God.

## 284. Does a fuller account of the Trinity drawn from Scripture make the doctrine more obscure because it multiplies the mysteries in view?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): No. It increases the number of visible mysteries only because God is seen to have told us more about Himself than was generally noticed. ## 298. Would a perfect reflexive idea of one's own mind amount to a second instance of oneself?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. ## 299. Is the reflexive or contemplative idea of oneself the same as mere consciousness?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): No. A reflexive idea is a view of oneself such that one can delight in one's own beauty or excellency, which differs greatly from mere direct consciousness.   > "There is a great difference between a man's having a view of himself, reflex or contemplative idea of himself so as to delight in his own beauty or excellency, and a mere direct consciousness." *[quote exact]*

## 305. From what is the name "Godhead" (θεότης) derived, and what does it signify?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): It is derived from an operation, namely θέα, beholding.   > "we suppose that Godhead, or θεότης, is so called from θέα, or beholding, and that He who is our θεατής or beholder, by customary use and by the instruction of the Scriptures, is called θεός, or God" *[quote exact]*

## 306. Are the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit three Gods or one God?

*theological*

The Catholic expounders of Scripture teach that Father, Son and Holy Spirit intimate a divine unity of one and the same substance in an indivisible equality. Therefore they are not three Gods but one God, and Augustine affirms this as his own faith because it is the Catholic faith.

## 308. Does Scripture explicitly call the Son equal with God the Father?

## 310. Since the Father is named in distinction from the Son in texts about the one God, does it follow that the Son is not the one God?

*logical*

- **Daniel Waterland, *A Vindication of Christ's Divinity*** (Query 2: Whether the texts of the New Testament (in the second column) do): No. The distinct naming proves only that the Persons are distinct, which tells against the Sabellians, who make one Person of two. ## 311. If the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are each, on the warrant of Scripture, objects of the supreme and undivided trust that belongs to the one infinite God alone, does it follow that all three are equally God?

*theological*

- **Edward Henry Bickersteth, *The Rock of Ages; or, Scripture Testimony to the One Eternal Godhead (later: The Trinity)*** (Preface): Yes. It follows that Father, Son, and Spirit are equally God over all, the Triune Jehovah, in whom alone we trust and whom alone we adore. Scripture: Isaiah 26:4.

## 312. How are the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit each to be understood as modes of the Deity subsisting?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): The Father is the Deity subsisting in the prime, unoriginated and most absolute manner, the Deity in its direct existence. The Son is the Deity generated by God's understanding, God's idea of himself in which the Deity subsists. The Holy Spirit is the Deity subsisting in act, the divine essence flowing out and breathed forth in God's infinite love to and delight in himself. The whole divine essence truly and distinctly subsists in both the divine idea and the divine love, so each is a properly distinct Person.
  > "The Son is the Deity generated by God's understanding, or having an idea of Himself and subsisting in that idea. The Holy Ghost is the Deity subsisting in act, or the Divine essence flowing out and breathed forth in God's Infinite love to and delight in Himself." *[quote exact]*

## 313. Since salvation is confessed to come from the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, are there three Saviours, and does this show why there are not three Gods?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): There are not three Saviours. Scripture speaks of one Saviour of all men, yet no one infers that the Son does not save or that salvation comes without the Spirit. God over all saves, while the Son works salvation by the grace of the Spirit. By the same reasoning the Trinity is not called three Gods, even though the name of Godhead belongs to each. Scripture: 1 Timothy 4:10.

## 314. If the Father is God and the Son is God, why are they not two Gods?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead. Plurality of Gods would follow only if the essence of the subjects were diverse. Since the divine nature is single and unchanging and rejects all diversity in essence, it does not admit the signification of multitude, and God is one.
  > "The Father is God: the Son is God: and yet by the same proclamation God is One, because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead." *[quote exact]*

## 315. Does the Father's declaration 'This is My beloved Son' rule out the claim that Christ is a creature rather than a true Son?

What pretence then for being indifferent in the controversy? The Arians rely on state patronage, and dare not avow their tenets.): Yes. Anyone who has put faith in God must believe what God says. ## 316. Does the Son's being called the radiance of the Father's glory prove that the Son is eternal?

## 317. Do Christ's sayings in the form 'I am' (the Truth, the Shepherd, the Light) show that the Son is eternal and without beginning?

*theological*

## 319. Does the worship (latreia) that Scripture says is paid to the Holy Spirit prove that the Spirit is not a creature?

*theological*

## 320. Does the fact that believers' bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit show that the Spirit is God and not a creature?

*exegetical*

- **Augustine of Hippo, *On the Holy Trinity (De Trinitate)*** (On the Holy Trinity. > The unity and equality of the Trinity are demonstrated out of the Scriptures; and the true interpretation is given of those texts which are wrongly alleged against the equality of the Son. > That the Son is Very God, of the Same Substance with the Father. ## 321. If Christ is God at all, must he be a different individual God from the Supreme God, the Father?

*logical*

## 324. Does 1 John 4:8-13 teach that the Holy Spirit is the love of God?

*exegetical*

Verse 13 shows the force of that argument by grounding it in God's having given us His Spirit, so the love that dwells in us is God's Spirit. ## 325. Do the Holy Spirit's title "Comforter" and the biblical phrase "joy in the Holy Ghost" support the idea that the Spirit is God's joy and delight?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. The Spirit's office of comforting and delighting God's people fits well with the Spirit being God's own joy and delight. Texts such as "joy of the Holy Ghost" and "filled with joy and with the Holy Ghost" mean that believers are filled with spiritual joy, which is the Spirit. ## 326. Why does 1 John 1:3 speak of fellowship with the Father and the Son but not with the Holy Spirit?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Our fellowship with the Father and the Son consists precisely in partaking with them of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit is therefore not a third party alongside them with whom we have fellowship. He is the common good, their love and joy, in which that fellowship consists. Scripture: 1 John 1:3.
  > "We are said to have fellowship with the Son and not with the Holy Ghost, because therein consists our fellowship with the Father and the Son, even in partaking with them of the Holy Ghost." *[quote exact]*

## 327. Is it unreasonable to say that a divine Person has understanding or love by another Person's being in Him?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): No. Scripture gives ground for it, since it teaches that the Son is the wisdom, reason and truth of God, so the Father is wise by His own wisdom, the Son, being in Him. Likewise understanding is in the Spirit because the Son is in Him. Scripture: 1 Corinthians 1:24, John 1:1, John 14:6.
  > "we have Scripture ground to conclude so concerning the Father's having wisdom and understanding or reason that it is by the Son's being in Him; because we are there informed that He is the wisdom and reason and truth of God" *[quote exact]*

## 328. Why is the Father so often called simply 'God' in Scripture, and does this mean only He is properly God?

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): The Father is called God without addition because He is the fountain of the Godhead and in the economy sustains the dignity of the Deity, upholding the rights of the Godhead; He is God not only by essence but by His economical office. This does not mean He alone is truly God, which is the mistaken inference some have drawn.
  > "Hereby we see how the Father is the fountain of the Godhead, and why when He is spoken of in Scripture He is so often, without any addition or distinction, called God, which has led some to think that He only was truly and properly God." *[quote exact]*

## 329. How can the Son be begotten of the Father and the Spirit proceed from Father and Son, while all three are co-eternal?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): On Edwards's model the Son is God's eternal idea or understanding of Himself and the Spirit is the eternal love between Father and Son; since God never lacks understanding or love, the begetting and procession involve no temporal priority, so all are co-eternal and the Spirit proceeds from both.
  > "Hereby we see how that it is possible for the Son to be begotten by the Father and the Holy Ghost to proceed from the Father and Son, and yet that all the Persons should be Co-etemal." *[quote exact]*

## 330. How does the sun serve as an image of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): The Father is like the sun's internal constitution or substance. The Son is like the brightness and glorious form of the sun's disk. The Spirit is like the sun's action: its internal heat represents God's infinite love to and happiness in himself, and its emitted beams represent God communicating himself to the world.
  > "The Spirit as it is God's Infinite love to Himself and happiness in Himself, is as the internal heat of the Sun, but as it is that by which God communicates Himself, it is as the emanation of the sun's action, or the emitted beams of the sun." *[quote exact]*

## 331. What do divine attributes such as "incorruptible" or "Giver of life" actually tell us about God?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): They either forbid us to conceive what we ought not of God or teach us what we ought to conceive, but they do not explain the nature itself. "Incorruptible" declares what His nature does not suffer, not what it is that does not suffer corruption; "Giver of life" shows what He gives, not what the giver is.
  > "when we say that He is incorruptible, we declare what His nature does not suffer, but we do not express what that is which does not suffer corruption" *[quote exact]*

## 332. Does the operation of beholding, from which the name "God" is derived, belong to only one Person of the Trinity or to all three?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): It extends to all three Persons, so none can be excluded from the name "God." Scripture attributes seeing equally to the Father ("See, O God our defender"), to the Son ("Jesus, seeing their thoughts"), and to the Holy Spirit, who detected Ananias' secret sin and gave Peter the power to see it. Scripture: Psalm 84:9, Matthew 9:4.
  > "no one of the Persons in the Trinity could reasonably be excluded from such an appellation on the ground of the sense involved in the word. For Scripture attributes the act of seeing equally to Father, Son, and Holy Spirit." *[quote exact]*

## 333. Does the episode of Ananias and Sapphira show that the Holy Spirit possesses the divine power of beholding hidden things?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. Ananias concealed his theft from all men, yet the Holy Spirit, present in Peter, detected his intent and gave Peter from Himself the power to see the secret, which He could not have done unless He could behold hidden things. So Peter's words to Ananias show the Spirit to be a true witness of secret deeds. Scripture: Acts 5:3.
  > "gave to Peter from Himself the power of seeing the secret, while it is clear that He could not have done this had He not been able to behold hidden things" *[quote exact]*

## 334. Since Scripture says the gift of life comes from the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, do we receive three lives or three Givers of life?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Though there are three Persons and three names, one and the same life is wrought in us. It is wrought by the Father, prepared by the Son, and depends on the will of the Spirit. So we do not speak of three Givers of life, nor of three Good beings, nor use the plural for any other divine attribute.
  > "we do not consider that we have had bestowed upon us three lives, one from each Person separately; but the same life is wrought in us by the Father, and prepared by the Son, and depends on the will of the Holy Spirit." *[quote exact]*

## 335. Does Scripture contradict itself when it says God judges all the earth and also that the Father judges no man?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. The Father judges all the earth through the Son, to whom He has committed all judgment. Everything the Son does refers back to the Father, and all the Son's judgment conforms to the Father's will. So the Father is at once Judge of all and judges no man. There are not two judges, and neither Person is excluded from the authority to judge. Scripture: Genesis 18:25, John 5:22.
  > "He Who judges all the earth does this by His Son to Whom He has committed all judgment; and everything which is done by the Only-begotten has its reference to the Father" *[quote exact]*

## 336. Does the Son act through the Holy Spirit, as shown by Isaiah's 'Spirit of judgment' and Christ's casting out devils 'by the Spirit of God'?

*exegetical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. The Father exercises the power of superintendence through the Only-begotten, and the Son perfects every power by the Holy Spirit. He judges by the Spirit of judgment and casts out devils by the Spirit of God. That single instance stands for every form of doing good, because the Persons' action is one. Scripture: Isaiah 4:4, Matthew 12:28, 1 Corinthians 1:24.
  > "where He includes every form of doing good in a partial description, by reason of the unity of action: for the name derived from operation cannot be divided among many where the result of their mutual operation is one." *[quote exact]*

## 337. Does the Church refuse to speak of 'three Gods' merely to avoid looking like pagan polytheism?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No, that explanation is inadequate. It might satisfy guileless people, but it gives no real solution to those who demand that we either deny the Godhead of the Three Persons or call them three Gods. A fuller reply grounded in the truth about the divine nature and how names signify is required.
  > "in the case of the others who require that one of the alternatives they propose should be established" *[quote exact]*

## 338. If human reasoning cannot resolve a difficulty about the Trinity, should the received faith be abandoned?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. Even if our reasoning proves unequal to the problem, the tradition received by succession from the fathers must be kept firm and unmoved. We should seek from the Lord a reason that defends the faith, and if none is found, we still hold the faith unchangeably.
  > "we must keep for ever, firm and unmoved, the tradition which we received by succession from the fathers, and seek from the Lord the reason which is the advocate of our faith" *[quote exact]*

## 339. Is a common nature such as humanity multiplied or divided when it exists in many individuals?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. The nature is one, united in itself, an indivisible unit not increased by addition or diminished by subtraction. It remains one and continuous even though it appears in plurality, and it is not divided among the individuals who share in it. Only the persons, separated by their peculiar attributes, are counted by number.
  > "yet their nature is one, at union in itself, and an absolutely indivisible unit, not capable of increase by addition or of diminution by subtraction" *[quote exact]*

## 340. What distinguishes individual persons who share one nature, such as Luke and Stephen?

*philosophical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Persons are distinguished by the peculiar attributes considered in each, and are signified by proper names rather than by the name of the nature. Luke is a man and Stephen is a man, but being a man does not make one Luke or Stephen. Persons can therefore be counted, while the nature remains one.
  > "the idea of the persons admits of that separation which is made by the peculiar attributes considered in each severally, and when they are combined is presented to us by means of number" *[quote exact]*

## 341. Can a collective noun in the singular properly name a plurality of individuals sharing one nature?

*logical*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. We say "a people," "a mob," "an army" or "an assembly" in the singular, even though each is a plurality.   > "as we speak of a people, or a mob, or an army, or an assembly in the singular in every case, while each of these is conceived as being in plurality, so according to the more accurate expression, “man” would be said to be one" *[quote exact]*

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. The motion of the divine will from the Father, through the Son, to the Spirit has no delay, either existent or conceived. Good things are brought to perfection without mark of time or distinction.

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): Yes. As the divine nature is called one nature, so it is called in the singular by all its other names. This holds whether the names refer to nature or to operation, a point Gregory declines to dispute.
  > "as it is called one nature, so it is called in the singular by all its other names, “God,” “Good,” “Holy,” “Saviour,” “Just,” “Judge,” and every other Divine name conceivable" *[quote exact]*

## 344. Does denying any difference of nature among Father, Son, and Spirit confuse or mix the Persons?

*theological*

- **Gregory of Nyssa, *On "Not Three Gods" (To Ablabius)*** (On 'Not Three Gods.'): No. While confessing the invariable character of the nature, we affirm a difference in respect of cause and what is caused. This difference alone distinguishes one Person from another: one is the Cause and another is of the Cause. Therefore community of nature does not confound the Persons.
  > "while we confess the invariable character of the nature, we do not deny the difference in respect of cause, and that which is caused, by which alone we apprehend that one Person is distinguished from another" *[quote exact]*

*philosophical*

## 388. Do God's essential holiness and justice indispensably require that sin be punished, so that God could not simply pardon sin without any satisfaction?

*theological*

- **John Owen, *A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*** (To the Reader): Yes. *theological*

*exegetical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. The various colored rays of the sun represent the love and grace of God and the various graces of the Spirit. Scripture uses them this way: in the rainbow after the flood, around God's throne and around Christ's head, and in the dove's silver and gold feathers in Psalm 68. Edwards holds that the Creator designed them for this purpose, as the visible creation is made to typify spiritual things. Scripture: Ezekiel 1:28, Revelation 4:3, Revelation 10:1, Psalm 68:13, Revelation 21, Isaiah 54:11, 1 Chronicles 29:2.
  > "These beautiful colors of the sunbeams we find made use of in Scripture for this purpose, viz., to represent the graces of the Spirit" *[quote exact]*

## 410. How is the Son begotten within the Godhead?

*philosophical*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): The Son is begotten by God's reflex act of knowledge, in which God views and knows Himself and so knows His own knowledge. In God there is knowledge of knowledge, an idea of an idea. This can only be God's knowledge or idea repeated, and that repeated idea is the Son.
  > "Then comes a reflex act of knowledge and His viewing Himself and knowing Himself and so knowing His own knowledge and so the Son is begotten. There is such a thing in God as knowledge of knowledge, an idea of an idea." *[quote exact]*

## 411. Was the world made especially for the Son of God?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. God made the world for Himself out of love to Himself, but He loves Himself only by a reflex act of viewing Himself. So He makes the world for Himself as viewed and reflected on. That is God repeated or begotten in His own idea, which is the Son. To make the world for the Godhead so understood is to make it for the Son.
  > "to make the world for the Godhead thus viewed and understood is to make the world for the Godhead begotten and that is to make the world for the Son of God" *[quote exact]*

## 412. Since Christ is the Divine wisdom, is the creation of the world directed toward gratifying the love in Christ's heart by providing Him a spouse?

*theological*

- **Jonathan Edwards, *An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity*** (An Unpublished Essay on the Trinity): Yes. God's love flowing outward is wholly directed by Divine wisdom, which chooses its objects. Christ is the Divine wisdom, so the world is made to gratify Divine love as exercised by Christ. That means it is made to provide a spouse for Christ. The elect creatures whom wisdom chooses are the end for which the rest of creation exists.
  > "But Christ is Divine wisdom so that the world is made to gratify Divine love as exercised by Christ or to gratify the love that is in Christ's heart, or to provide a spouse for Christ." *[quote exact]*
